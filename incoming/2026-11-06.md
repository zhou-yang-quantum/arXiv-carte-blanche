## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 1

### Provable circuit-level thresholds for non-abelian topological phases
**Yuanjie Ren, Margarita Davydova, Shankar Balasubramanian, Andreas Bauer — arXiv:2611.08488**

Non-Abelian anyons offer a route to logical operations through their braiding, but a computational scheme also needs repeated syndrome extraction and recovery that tolerate faults in those very operations. For general non-Abelian phases, the connection between a topological description and an end-to-end fault-tolerant circuit has remained incomplete.

The authors construct this connection for two-dimensional non-chiral bosonic topological orders described by Turaev–Viro state sums. They turn the spacetime path integral into local quantum channels that extract syndrome information and move excitations. A classical decoder clusters the observed anyon worldlines and gradually brings each cluster together for annihilation. Correction proceeds alongside measurement, allowing later information to repair earlier decisions.

They prove a nonzero threshold under local stochastic circuit-level noise. Below it, the memory lifetime grows quasi-stretched-exponentially with system size. The construction assumes sufficiently fast classical processing, and the paper also develops extensions to topological logical operations.

**Background.** A Turaev–Viro state sum assigns amplitudes to a triangulated spacetime using local fusion data. Its consistency identities make local changes of triangulation compatible with the same topological evolution. Here those identities also show that damage confined to a contractible region cannot enact a logical operation. Non-Abelian fusion stores information in how excitations combine, so recovery must track their evolving worldlines and physically manipulate them. Moving each excitation only a bounded distance per round limits the damage caused by a mistaken measurement record.

**Why it matters?** This establishes a general bridge from non-Abelian topological order to circuit-level fault tolerance. Its significance is primarily foundational. Quantitative thresholds and implementation costs will determine practical competitiveness, while the construction already broadens the theoretical basis of topological QEC substantially.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 2

### Threshold-Free Symmetry-Enriched Topological Quantum Memory
**Abhijat Sarma, Ruochen Ma, Cenke Xu — arXiv:2611.07017**

Can additional symmetry information make a topological memory recoverable even when its error strings become dense? Ordinary toric-code syndromes reveal endpoint parity, leaving many possible paths indistinguishable. This paper enriches the syndrome by attaching symmetry-carrying matter to the code.

The construction places symplectic-group rotors on the vertices of a honeycomb toric code. An electric error also deposits fundamental symmetry representations at its endpoints. Measuring the local representation therefore reveals information about how many activated error edges meet at a vertex. At infinite representation dimension, that degree is determined exactly. Competing error configurations then differ along alternating loops whose probabilities decrease sufficiently rapidly to overcome the number of possible loop shapes.

At finite dimension, fusion can hide pairs of incident errors. The authors bound these ambiguities and construct a polynomial-time minimum-weight decoder with exponentially decreasing logical failure for every bond activation probability $p\in[0,1]$, provided $N\ge40$. The bound is conservative. The result concerns the specified symmetry-preserving incoherent electric channel and its representation measurements.

**Background.** The rotors supply local states transforming in representations of the continuous symmetry. Multiple incident errors combine these representations according to fusion rules. For large $N$, accidental fusion back into a low-dimensional representation is rare, so representation measurements carry more information than an anyon-presence bit. The paper uses the convention that $N$ is the even dimension of the fundamental representation. The honeycomb geometry is important because its loop-counting growth rate permits the required probability bound.

**Why it matters?** This gives an explicit mechanism through which symmetry enrichment changes the qualitative decodability phase diagram. It connects representation theory, error heralding, and statistical-mechanical loop bounds. Extending protection to imperfect representation readout and broader noise channels would establish how much of this mechanism survives in a more general memory architecture.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 3

### Extensions of the Haldane gap proof: higher dimensions, near degeneracy, fermions and tensor-network states
**Tarun Grover — arXiv:2611.07400**

Finite-system spectra can strongly suggest a gapped phase, yet extrapolation alone leaves open what happens at arbitrarily large size. The difficulty becomes sharper when several low-energy states approach degeneracy while remaining separated from excitations. This paper extends a finite-size certification method to handle that structure.

The method compares purities associated with a thermal partition function and with its spatial transfer operator. Inequalities propagate sufficiently strong finite-size bounds through successive doublings of length and inverse temperature. Grover combines this procedure with symmetry twists, which distinguish the sectors needed to isolate a low-energy multiplet.

For explicit parameter ranges, finite calculations certify four low states in the toric code in a field and two in the transverse-field Ising model, together with a nonzero gap above them. Their spreads are bounded respectively by $e^{-cL}$ and $e^{-cL^2}$, with positive size-independent $c$. Further applications cover symmetric mass generation in a fermionic model and exponential correlation decay in a square-lattice tensor-network state. The method requires suitable Hermitian spatial transfer representations and successful finite purity tests.

**Background.** Exchanging space and imaginary time rewrites the same partition function as propagation along a spatial direction. The resulting transfer spectrum defines a second probability distribution. Squaring and renormalizing a nearly pure distribution makes it still purer, allowing a bootstrap between these two descriptions. A symmetry twist inserts a symmetry transformation across a boundary. Space-time exchange relates such twists to symmetry-sector projections, enabling separate control of several nearly degenerate states.

**Why it matters?** The contribution turns selected finite computations into thermodynamic statements with controlled gaps and splittings. It is a potentially powerful certification framework for topological and symmetry-broken phases. Establishing the transfer assumptions and obtaining tight finite partition-function bounds remain the main model-dependent tasks.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 4

### Conformal Bootstrap Unblocked
**Li-Yuan Chiang, David Poland — arXiv:2611.07004**

How much of the conformal bootstrap follows directly from Hilbert-space positivity and symmetry? This question matters both conceptually and computationally, particularly when constraining correlators across different insertion geometries becomes cumbersome.

The authors prepare states using pairs of local operators and build their Gram matrices. Conformal Ward identities express the matrix elements through a four-point function and its derivatives. Reflection positivity requires these matrices to be positive semidefinite, while crossing relates the correlator data. Additional positive operators constructed from conformal generators impose spectral assumptions. Enlarging the probe-state space gives a hierarchy of semidefinite programs.

Tests in one to three dimensions recover nontrivial correlator and spectral bounds, including characteristic Ising kinks. Joint positivity of states prepared at different configurations strengthens bounds away from crossing-symmetric kinematics. The present implementation is computationally less efficient than established bootstrap methods because the matrices grow rapidly with derivative order.

**Background.** A Gram matrix tests the norm of every linear combination of a chosen set of states. Negative eigenvalues would imply an unphysical negative-norm state. Spectral assumptions enter through similarly constructed matrices with an inserted operator that is positive on the allowed spectrum. For example, a lower bound on dimensions makes the dilatation generator minus that bound positive on the relevant states. Carefully chosen combinations of generators distinguish primaries from descendants, permitting more selective gap constraints.

**Why it matters?** This is a conceptual and methodological reformulation of nonperturbative QFT consistency. Its mixed-configuration construction gives a particularly natural way to connect correlator data at separated kinematic points. Numerical improvements and a fuller understanding of the infinite-hierarchy limit will determine its eventual reach.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 5

### Fidelity Determines the Minimum Error of Quantum Recovery
**Dario Picozzi — arXiv:2611.07344**

A recovery channel can score well under entanglement fidelity while retaining a coherent rotation that changes subsequent measurement probabilities appreciably. General fidelity–distance inequalities allow a square-root separation. The paper asks whether that separation persists after optimizing recovery.

For finite-dimensional noise and a fixed encoding, the author proves equality between the minimum normalized diamond error and the minimum worst-input entanglement infidelity. The central construction appends one logical unitary to an existing decoder. A worst-preserved input identifies a coherent combination of its Kraus amplitudes, and polar decomposition separates that combination into a rotation and a positive filter. Cancelling the rotation bounds the corrected operational error by the original infidelity.

Writing $F$ for worst-input squared entanglement fidelity and $D$ for normalized diamond distance, the unrestricted recovery optimum obeys $\min D=1-\max F$. Restricted decoder families satisfy the same relation when they are closed under appending output unitaries. The paper also treats retained noise records and rank constraints.

**Background.** The weighted Kraus combination is selected by the input that minimizes fidelity. This choice is essential because a correction based on a single arbitrarily chosen Kraus operator need not control the worst case. Polar decomposition extracts a unitary from the combined amplitude. After its removal, the remaining channel admits a positive-filter description with a bounded residual contribution. Computing this correction requires characterization of the decoded channel.

**Why it matters?** This sharpens the operational meaning of recovery-fidelity optimization and identifies a deterministic calibration step that can make the optimized guarantee attainable. For QEC with coherent residual errors, it clarifies how decoder optimization and final logical calibration fit together. Efficient characterization and implementation remain separate resource questions.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 6

### Single-shot ground-state preparation via strongly coupled baths
**Liang-Hong Mo, Jerome Lloyd, Yuxuan Zhang, Dmitry A. Abanin, Biao Lian — arXiv:2611.07021**

Dissipative state preparation often cools slowly because weak coupling limits excitation removal. Strong coupling can accelerate transfer, but also produces unwanted heating. This paper designs the coupling and bath spectrum together to control that competition.

Resettable ancillas have energy levels swept through the system spectrum while their couplings are smoothly shaped. For a class of free-fermion systems, the dynamics reduces exactly to two-level scattering problems. Matching the sweep speed to the squared coupling makes resonant transfer approximately uniform across energies. Smooth control functions then suppress off-resonant heating through their Fourier tails.

The resulting Möbius–Ingham protocols achieve the derived optimal error scaling for gapless spectra and almost-exponential suppression of the leading heating contribution for gapped spectra as cycle duration grows. Benchmarks include interacting chains and a two-dimensional Fermi–Hubbard model, where the evidence is numerical. The authors also extend the construction to free-fermion thermal-state preparation. A single cycle can approach the protocol's steady-state accuracy, with a duration and ancilla budget that remain physical resources.

**Background.** The scattering problem has a cooling sector, where an ancilla crossing should transfer an excitation, and a heating sector, where transitions should be suppressed. Strong resonant transfer addresses the first requirement. The smoothness of a finite-duration pulse controls its high-frequency Fourier content and hence the second. The special control family coordinates these two requirements while maintaining a sweep appropriate to the coupling envelope.

**Why it matters?** The work provides an analytically organized route to fast bath-assisted preparation beyond weak coupling. Its strongest results explain which control features determine the error–time trade-off. The interacting benchmarks make the method promising for quantum simulation, while broader guarantees would require control of interacting spectral and transition structure.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 7

### Certifying quantum code and circuit distances at scale
**Bin Cheng, Naixu Guo, Feng Pan — arXiv:2611.08247**

Finding a low-weight logical fault establishes an upper bound on distance. Certifying distance additionally requires excluding every lighter fault. This distinction becomes consequential when architectural resource estimates rely on heuristic searches or on an assumed syndrome-extraction schedule.

The authors formulate code and circuit distances as minimum-weight solutions of binary constraints and evaluate them using rank-based dynamic programming, weight-bounded search, and certified relaxations. Exact values come with a lower bound and a matching explicit logical error. Circuit witnesses are replayed in simulation.

The method reaches codes with 31,200 qubits and circuits with hundreds of thousands of fault mechanisms. An audit finds overestimates or loose estimates in ten of twenty-six papers. A concrete design consequence is that the standard two-gross memory schedule has certified circuit distance 16 in the studied setting, while redesigned schedules reach 18 for one noisy cycle with an additional timestep.

**Background.** A tropical partition function replaces addition of statistical weights by minimization and multiplication by addition of costs. It therefore computes the lightest allowed fault directly. Dynamic programming merges partial assignments whenever they have the same remaining boundary constraints, retaining only their minimum weight. The relevant computational width measures how much independent constraint information crosses a decomposition cut. This explains why some very large sparse instances remain tractable.

**Why it matters?** This is a substantial verification and design tool for QEC. It makes distance claims auditable and exposes how circuit scheduling changes protection even for a fixed code. The algorithms retain instance-dependent costs, but the demonstrated scale supports applications well beyond small-code validation.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 8

### Nonlocal magic of fermions is bounded by entanglement fluctuations and is logarithmic for free fermions
**Raghav G. Jha, Dengyao Luo, Alexander F. Kemper, Bojko N. Bakalov — arXiv:2611.07003**

Free-fermion states are efficiently simulable but can carry extensive qubit magic. To understand which part of that resource is genuinely shared across a bipartition, one must minimize over operations confined to each side. The answer depends strongly on which local operations are allowed.

Restricting to Gaussian local unitaries gives nonlocal magic proportional to the number of partially entangled pairs. The authors show that general parity-preserving local unitaries reduce it to logarithmic growth. Gaussian operations are optimal for up to three pairs, but cease to be optimal from four onward.

The mechanism is a rearrangement of Schmidt weights toward a flat spectrum associated with Bell pairs. A universal bound relates the remaining nonlocal magic to the capacity of entanglement, the variance of the entanglement Hamiltonian. Capacities add for independent free-fermion pairs, yielding a stabilizer-overlap guarantee that decreases only algebraically with pair number.

**Background.** Gaussian unitaries act through linear transformations of fermionic modes and preserve Gaussian structure. A general parity-preserving unitary can rearrange many-body basis states within each parity sector, accessing transformations unavailable to quadratic dynamics. The capacity measures fluctuations of entanglement energies weighted by their Schmidt probabilities. A flat entanglement spectrum has zero capacity even when its entropy is large, making it an appropriate measure of departure from Bell-pair structure.

**Why it matters?** This changes the resource-theoretic interpretation of free-fermion magic and highlights the importance of the allowed local operation class. The useful connection is between nonstabilizerness and fluctuations of entanglement structure. Turning the optimizing transformations into efficient circuits would require additional algorithmic results.

### Observable-Based Learning of Effective Entanglement Hamiltonians
**Siyi Yang, Yuzhi Liu, Yi-Ming Ding, Zheng Yan — arXiv:2611.07353**

The entanglement Hamiltonian organizes a subsystem's full entanglement spectrum, but extracting it in an interacting two-dimensional system is difficult. Continuum-inspired spatial profiles provide useful guesses, yet lattice inhomogeneity and entanglement-boundary physics can distort them.

The authors fit an effective entanglement Hamiltonian by matching local observables measured in the original state. Quantum Monte Carlo estimates both trial observables and their responses to changes in couplings, supplying the optimization gradient. They first calibrate a lattice Bisognano–Wichmann ansatz, then allow the couplings to vary spatially beyond its prescribed profile.

Entropies withheld from training provide independent benchmarks. At a dimerized Heisenberg critical point with a strong-bond cut, free-form learning improves the second Rényi entropy and reveals spatially nonuniform coupling renormalizations. The learned model also yields a von Neumann entropy estimate with a negative subleading correction. Its accuracy depends on the chosen operator basis and quality of observable matching.

**Background.** The lattice Bisognano–Wichmann construction weights local Hamiltonian terms according to their distance from the entanglement cut, motivated by the continuum modular Hamiltonian. Learning relaxes those weights while retaining a tractable local operator basis. Coupling derivatives are integrated imaginary-time connected correlations, so the sampling distribution's response must be included. Once the fitted model is available, entanglement observables become thermal observables of that effective Hamiltonian.

**Why it matters?** This offers a practical route from accessible correlations to otherwise difficult entanglement information in interacting systems. The boundary-critical benchmark is especially informative because it tests where a simple continuum profile loses accuracy. Extending the operator basis and testing additional independent observables would strengthen confidence in applications beyond the demonstrated models.

### Exact and Near-Exact Inverse Renormalization Group with Constrained Monte Carlo
**Dimitrios Bachtis — arXiv:2611.07641**

Near criticality, local Monte Carlo struggles to build large-scale fluctuations efficiently. Starting with independent coarse configurations and adding short-distance structure offers another route, provided that the refinement samples the correct fine-lattice distribution.

This paper formulates refinement as constrained Monte Carlo. Fine variables fluctuate while remaining compatible with fixed coarse block variables under a specified forward transformation. The author shows that the inversion is exact when the input coarse distribution matches the appropriate marginal, as it does at a fixed-point action. Away from that situation, the distributional mismatch originates in the coarse variables.

An optimized transformation is applied to three-dimensional scalar quartic theory, expanding independent configurations from linear size 16 to 128 and extracting critical exponents. The measured dynamic exponent is $z=0.03(2)$, consistent with negligible critical slowing down in this implementation. The large-volume samples are described as near-equilibrium, reflecting the distinction between the exact conditional construction and its numerical fixed-point approximation.

**Background.** Coarse graining loses microscopic information, so its inverse is a conditional distribution over compatible fine configurations. Given the correct coarse marginal, sampling that conditional reconstructs the full equilibrium ensemble. An incorrect coarse marginal leaves an error even if the conditional is sampled exactly. The paper bounds the final relative-entropy mismatch by the input mismatch, making the source of approximation explicit.

**Why it matters?** The work clarifies when inverse RG sampling is exact and supplies a concrete critical-field-theory demonstration. Its main methodological value is combining a transparent probabilistic condition with an optimized refinement scheme. Controlled coarse distributions and efficient constrained updates will govern its usefulness in more complicated theories.

**Frank Göhmann, Karol K. Kozlowski, Mikhail D. Minin, Alexander Weiße — arXiv:2611.08350**

Exact spectra do not automatically give transparent dynamical correlations. The impenetrable Bose gas illustrates this sharply, since its thermodynamics maps to free fermions while its bosonic field correlator retains a nonlocal string structure.

The authors derive explicit long-time, large-distance asymptotics for this correlator in stationary states specified by a momentum filling function. These include thermal and generalized Gibbs ensembles. The leading answer separates a relaxation rate, logarithmic contributions to the exponent, an oscillatory phase, and an amplitude. For the class with two Fermi points, they also calculate the first subleading correction.

The analysis organizes the answer by how often the filling crosses one half. The strongest results concern smooth, sufficiently decaying fillings with zero or two such crossings, while split-sea configurations receive a separate treatment. This converts determinant representations into formulas whose physical dependence on the stationary state can be inspected directly.

**Background.** Here a Fermi point means a momentum where the filling equals one half, extending the terminology beyond a zero-temperature occupation edge. These crossings produce singular structure in the auxiliary functions controlling the determinant asymptotics. The correlator is represented by a Fredholm determinant and minor, the continuum counterparts of determinants built from a kernel. Their asymptotic analysis distinguishes saddle-point propagation from contributions associated with the filling crossings.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful exact-results contribution for integrable dynamics. Explicit amplitudes and correction terms make comparisons at accessible distances and times more meaningful than leading decay rates alone. Generalized stationary fillings also connect the calculation to correlation dynamics after integrable relaxation.

### Exponential mixing time for the Davies dynamics of the quantum random energy model
**Giacomo De Palma, Emanuele Mingione — arXiv:2611.08064**

A frozen equilibrium landscape suggests slow relaxation, but a quantum bath induces transitions between energy eigenstates, and transverse-field tunnelling can change the classical bottleneck picture. A rigorous dynamical conclusion therefore needs control of both spectral localization and bath-induced matrix elements.

The authors provide that control for the quantum random energy model coupled through single-qubit Pauli operators to a Davies bath. Throughout the equilibrium spin-glass region below its quantum transition line, they prove an exponentially large mixing time, almost surely in the disorder.

Deep configurations carrying appreciable Gibbs weight lie far apart in Hamming distance. Low-energy localization transfers this separation to quantum eigenstates. Intermediate spectral sectors have exponentially small Gibbs weight, while local bath operators have exponentially small matrix elements across the bottleneck. A flux estimate converts these facts into the mixing-time bound. It holds uniformly for detailed-balance rates bounded above polynomially in system size.

**Background.** Davies dynamics describes the weak-coupling Markovian limit of a thermal environment. Each coupling operator is resolved into transitions at the system's energy differences. Consequently, a single-qubit coupling can connect complicated eigenstates, and its transition strengths require explicit analysis. Mixing time measures loss of dependence on the initial state under the full open-system evolution. The random energy model's diagonal potential is nonlocal in real-space terms, although bath couplings act on individual qubits.

**Why it matters?** This establishes a rigorous dynamical consequence of quantum glass freezing and identifies why local thermal contact cannot efficiently bypass it. It provides a useful obstruction for dissipative preparation and quantum annealing intuition. Extending the argument to spatially local glass models would be a substantial next step.

### Polynomial-time classical simulation of boson and fermion sampling under thermal loss
**Armando Angrisani, Hela Mhiri — arXiv:2611.08444**

Particle-sampling proposals derive their computational power from high-order interference. Understanding which of those correlations survive realistic loss is therefore central to assessing scalable quantum advantage.

The authors develop a common simulation framework for Fock-state boson sampling, uniformly squeezed Gaussian boson sampling, and fermion sampling with specified non-Gaussian magic inputs. For Haar-random interferometers, any fixed nonzero homogeneous loss rate admits polynomial-time approximate classical sampling with high probability, including inverse-polynomial total-variation accuracy. Thermal noise is included.

The mechanism expands occupation statistics into contributions of increasing polynomial degree. Loss suppresses the higher-degree information, allowing truncation, and the authors convert the resulting approximation into a controlled sampler. A weaker flatness assumption gives quasipolynomial simulation for general interferometers. The runtime bounds grow rapidly as loss decreases, leaving finite-size practical costs important.

**Background.** Degree labels isolate many-particle occupation correlations after subtracting lower-degree contributions. The relevant polynomial basis depends on particle statistics and the input family, while the suppression mechanism is shared. Flatness controls how concentrated the ideal distribution is relative to a tractable reference. It prevents the truncated expansion's average error from becoming large. Haar averaging also enables efficient randomized evaluation of the retained contributions.

**Why it matters?** This materially sharpens the asymptotic noise constraints on several sampling architectures. The physical conclusion is that retaining a constant fraction of particles can coexist with efficient asymptotic simulation in the covered ensembles. Structured interferometers, peaked distributions, and achievable finite-size simulation costs remain important boundaries of the result.

### Non-Unitary Quantum Butterfly Effect: Theory and Experiment
**Paul Argyle, Djamil Lakhdar-Hamina, Sarah H. Miller, Victor Galitski — arXiv:2611.08152**

A local perturbation can spread through a monitored circuit even as measurement erases its observable influence. Separating propagation from attenuation requires a scrambling diagnostic compatible with general quantum channels.

The authors define such a diagnostic from the change in a backward-evolved probe under a local perturbation channel. Its squared expectation gives a generalized out-of-time-ordered correlator and bounds the corresponding change in measured response. For unitary evolution and perturbation, it recovers the usual squared-commutator construction.

They calculate the infinite-temperature ensemble average for Haar-random brickwork circuits with local projective measurements using a transfer matrix. The signal develops a causal front but decays behind it, with an asymptotic exponential rate dependent on measurement strength. A sparse-measurement thermodynamic regime has an approximately Gaussian profile. Monte Carlo calculations and trapped-ion experiments support the predicted phenomenology.

**Background.** The adjoint of a quantum channel evolves observables backward through the circuit. Applying a local perturbation to that evolved observable reveals how sensitive the later probe is to an earlier disturbance. Squaring this response makes a positive diagnostic. Measurement is represented through a channel, so averaging and information loss enter its definition directly. This convention is important when comparing it with trajectory-conditioned scrambling diagnostics.

**Why it matters?** The work gives an operationally motivated benchmark for information spreading under nonunitary dynamics. Its controlled random-circuit calculation helps distinguish a moving influence front from the survival of its signal. Applications to hardware noise will require matching the experimental channel to the assumptions used in the analytic benchmark.

**Hugues Chaté, Alexandre Solon — arXiv:2611.07771**

**Background.** Shifting the orientation field changes the direction around which the moving flock is described. **Why it matters?** This provides a concrete example of emergent symmetry fixing nonequilibrium scaling beyond what bare power counting can determine. Its broader interest lies in the interplay between hydrodynamic truncation, symmetry restoration, and exact exponent relations. ### Constraining the spectral gap of 2d CFTs just above c=1
**Nathan Benjamin, Kevin Langhoff, Wei Li, Jesse Thaler — arXiv:2611.07011**

The familiar classification of low-central-charge CFTs leaves a striking question immediately above one. If compact unitary theories exist arbitrarily close to that boundary, what must their light spectra contain? Conventional modular bounds leave considerable freedom because they relax operator multiplicities to nonnegative real numbers.

This paper enforces their integrality. A primal search builds approximate discrete spectra, while a dual method bounds the number of primaries in selected dimension windows. A window count constrained between consecutive integers gives a rigorous exclusion.

For the parity-symmetric theories studied, the improvement becomes dramatic near the boundary. At $c-1=0.003$, the reported rigorous gap bound improves from roughly one half to below 0.041. The results suggest a closing primary gap as the central charge approaches one from above. Approximate primal spectra support the picture, while rigorous exclusions come from the dual analysis.

**Background.** Window integrality imposes a discrete state-counting requirement without first determining the entire spectrum. Modular invariance and linear functionals give upper and lower bounds on how many primaries can lie in a chosen interval at fixed spin. If no nonnegative integer fits, the assumed spectral gap is impossible. Opposite-spin primaries are counted as parity pairs under the paper's symmetry assumption.

**Why it matters?** This demonstrates how much physical information can be lost by a continuous relaxation of the spectrum. It substantially sharpens constraints in an unusually poorly understood part of two-dimensional QFT. Establishing the limiting behavior rigorously, or excluding an interval of central charges altogether, remains an open problem.

For a gapless bulk, energy exchange supplies correlators that can carry surface information away from the surface itself.

**Why it matters?** This gives a useful route to universal defect data through bulk observables and defect spectra. It could make anomaly constraints more accessible to numerical and bootstrap calculations, provided the required integrations and subtractions can be controlled. **Hao Geng, Zekai Wang — arXiv:2611.07001**

Low-energy chiral-fermion scattering from a monopole forces several descriptions to meet. Incoming and outgoing fermions have different charge assignments, the monopole's electric collective coordinate participates, and anomalous amplitudes involve fermion-number violation. Treating these ingredients separately can make charge and energy conservation appear difficult to reconcile.

The authors derive a common fermion–rotor description from a minimally charged BPS monopole background. Adding a decoupled auxiliary scalar enables an exactly quantizable dual formulation. The coupled rotor vertex has spectral support extending continuously to zero energy, resolving the energy obstruction suggested by an isolated gapped rotor.

Its infrared exchange relations identify the same vertex as the twist dressing attached to the outgoing fermion. Separately, compact-rotor winding configurations generate fermion zero modes that produce anomalous correlators. The paper thereby connects dynamical charge transfer, twisted outgoing states, and anomalous amplitudes within their common low-energy regime.

**Background.** The rotor is the monopole's periodic electric collective coordinate, with conjugate momentum carrying electric charge. Its coupling to gapless fermions changes the spectral content of a charge-changing vertex. A twist dressing alters exchange relations and identifies the outgoing excitation's sector. Rotor winding means that the periodic coordinate traverses a nontrivial cycle in a path-integral configuration, creating the background responsible for the relevant fermion zero modes.

**Why it matters?** This is a clarifying QFT contribution whose value lies in an explicit dictionary between descriptions that emphasize different physics. The solvable dual formulation supports that reconciliation at operator level. Together with the spin-chain demonstration above, it offers complementary analytic and simulation perspectives on defect-dressed scattering.

## arXiv-2026-11-06 — Item 20

### Universal Phase Locking from Slowly Varying Inhomogeneity in Tunnel-Coupled One-Dimensional Condensates
**Bence Fitos, Gábor Takács — arXiv:2611.07657**

Tunnel-coupled one-dimensional condensates realize sine-Gordon dynamics, but their observed late-time phase locking differs from the behavior of the ideal homogeneous integrable model. A smooth trapping profile seems weak locally, yet can accumulate a large dynamical effect.

The authors combine semiclassical simulations with asymptotic analysis to identify a curvature-controlled relaxation mechanism. Near the density maximum, the relative phase distribution drifts toward zero. This effect already occurs in the linearized inhomogeneous theory. Its long timescale is set by density curvature and is independent of tunnel coupling in the derived regime.

Rescaling time by that relaxation scale collapses dynamics across system sizes and profiles. Nonlinearity suppresses excitations returning from the edges and helps sustain the locking. The analysis distinguishes early Josephson-scale dephasing from the later trap-controlled behavior, with simulations in the weak-fluctuation, slowly varying regime.

**Background.** The truncated Wigner approximation samples quantum fluctuations in the initial conditions and evolves each sample with classical field equations. Here it is paired with a linearized treatment that isolates the origin of the slow drift. Phase locking refers to concentration of the local relative-phase distribution near zero, measured at the trap center. Its dynamics differs from that of the spatially averaged phase, so the choice of observable matters.

**Why it matters?** This explains how a smooth spatial profile can qualitatively change the late-time predictions of an integrable field-theory simulator. The separation of linear drift and nonlinear suppression of returning excitations gives a concrete mechanism to test. It also illustrates why controlled inhomogeneity deserves explicit treatment when connecting exact models to quantum-simulation experiments.
