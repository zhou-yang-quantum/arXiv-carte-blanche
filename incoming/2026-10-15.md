### Measurement-induced criticality for non-Abelian anyons: The monitored golden chain
**Zhaohui Zhi, Bo Han, Romain Vasseur, Andreas W. W. Ludwig, Simon Trebst, Guo-Yi Zhu — arXiv:2610.18965**

Local measurements can remove quantum uncertainty without driving a system toward an ordinary, short-range state. In an anyon chain they also confront a specifically non-Abelian obstacle: determining the fusion channel of one neighboring pair changes what can be known about an overlapping pair. The question is whether this competition sustains criticality, and how the resulting dynamics reveals a globally conserved topological charge to an observer who sees only local outcomes.

The authors replace the golden chain's energetically favored fusion channels by repeated measurements of neighboring Fibonacci anyons. Each outcome selects a quantum trajectory with its Born probability. They map these trajectories to a constrained random tensor network and compare several long-distance diagnostics: the low-lying Lyapunov gaps decrease inversely with chain length, entanglement grows logarithmically with chord distance, and the measurement record has a conformal finite-size contribution to its Shannon entropy. These numerical results support a non-unitary conformal description of the monitored problem.

The global charge becomes learnable on a time proportional to system size, consistent with $z=1$. Thus a conserved quantity can remain unchanged physically while becoming progressively less uncertain to the observer. The study supplies substantial finite-size evidence for the critical description and its persistence as measurement strength varies; it does not analytically identify the full continuum theory.

**Background.** Fibonacci fusion obeys $\tau\times\tau=\mathbf{1}+\tau$. A chain's degrees of freedom therefore include the possible intermediate charges obtained by successively combining its anyons. Reassociating that fusion tree changes the basis in which a neighboring-pair charge is diagonal. Alternating measurements on overlapping pairs consequently compete, even though each measurement is local.

The topological symmetry inserts an auxiliary anyon line around the chain. Such lines compose according to fusion rules, so their composition can be a sum of sectors rather than a single invertible group operation. This is the relevant non-invertible symmetry. In the monitored tensor network, Lyapunov exponents describe long-time growth rates of products of non-unitary transfer matrices; differences between them play the role that energy gaps play in a Hamiltonian finite-size analysis. Matching several diagnostics is stronger evidence for conformal behavior than fitting one entropy curve alone.

**Why it matters?** The work provides a concrete setting in which topological information, local measurement records, and non-unitary criticality can be studied together. It gives the learning problem an observable timescale, rather than identifying criticality solely from entanglement. For monitored quantum matter, the useful next step is to determine the continuum operator content and which perturbations the non-invertible symmetry actually excludes. Those questions would clarify how broadly this mechanism extends beyond the specific golden chain.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 2

### Instability of Markov chains under infinitesimal coherent dissipation
**Amir-Reza Negari, Yijian Zou, Timothy H. Hsieh — arXiv:2610.18983**

An exponentially decaying conditional-information tail is useful because it supports recovery using a nearby buffer. But this usefulness does not establish that its associated length is a stable label of a mixed-state phase. Negari, Zou, and Hsieh ask how abruptly finite Markov length can fail under local quantum dissipation, and whether that failure necessarily means that preparation or reversal has become operationally difficult.

Their starting point is an exactly solvable fermion chain, initially in the vacuum, with local jump operators $F_j=c_j^\dagger+c_{j+1}^\dagger$. A jump creates a coherent superposition on neighboring sites. The associated occupation spectrum has a zero, and the nonanalytic dependence of von Neumann entropy on a small occupation converts this local structure into algebraically decaying conditional mutual information. The paper establishes an algebraic lower bound at every fixed positive time, so no finite exponential Markov length describes the resulting tail, however short the evolution.

An auxiliary-mode construction transfers the phenomenon to a strictly local spin Lindbladian. Related examples rapidly approach steady states with algebraic conditional-information tails. Nevertheless, the constructed states remain approximately reversibly connected to a product state when sublinear locality and inverse-polynomial error are allowed. Stronger requirements on reversal accuracy lead to a different conclusion. The operational tolerance is therefore part of the statement, not a technicality that can be omitted.

**Background.** The important distinction is between the magnitude of a tail and its asymptotic decay law. A very small power-law contribution can be irrelevant at a chosen error tolerance while still defeating every fixed exponential bound at sufficiently large separation. Entropy is especially sensitive to this distinction because its dependence on an occupation eigenvalue contains $-x\log x$, which is nonanalytic at zero. Local evolution can therefore produce long-distance conditional-information tails without transmitting ordinary observables outside a Lieb–Robinson light cone.

The spin realization also needs care: a bare Jordan–Wigner image of a single fermion creation operator contains a long parity string. Introducing an auxiliary Majorana makes the jump parity even, allowing those strings to cancel outside a bounded region. This converts the solvable fermion mechanism into a genuinely local spin example rather than merely relabeling a nonlocal operator.

**Why it matters?** This challenges a proposed diagnostic at precisely the point where a structural criterion is used to infer operational complexity. It distinguishes infinite Markov length from slow relaxation and from the impossibility of useful approximate recovery. For mixed-state phases and local decoding, the consequential question becomes which decay bounds are sufficient at the accuracy and length scales that the task actually demands. The examples make that question concrete without establishing that every algebraic tail is harmless.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 3

### Invertible subalgebras for chiral semion topological order
**Yu-An Chen, Meng Sun, Nathanan Tantivasadakarn, Zongyuan Wang, Bowen Yang — arXiv:2610.18953**

Chiral topological order exposes a limitation of a familiar microscopic strategy: strictly two-dimensional commuting-projector Hamiltonians on ordinary finite-dimensional local tensor factors cannot realize nonzero chiral central charge. One alternative is to describe a chiral sector through its algebra of local observables. The challenge then is to make that algebra explicit and show how it fits into the complete microscopic operator algebra.

Starting with the $\mathbb{Z}_4$ toric code, the authors condense a bosonic charge-flux composite and identify complementary semion and anti-semion algebras. After disentangling an ancillary degree of freedom, the construction uses one qubit per square-lattice edge. Its generators are non-Pauli operators. The authors prove that the two commuting algebras jointly reconstruct every local microscopic operator with a bounded enlargement of support.

This local factorization also defines a three-dimensional non-Clifford quantum cellular automaton: one algebraic factor is transported between layers while the complementary factor is held fixed. Eight applications become a translation followed by a finite-depth circuit after adding ancillary degrees of freedom. The paper also connects the explicit construction to an earlier topological-field-theory description. These are statements about operator algebras and locality-preserving transformations; they do not supply an ordinary two-dimensional commuting-projector realization that evades the original obstruction.

**Background.** The commutant of an algebra contains all operators commuting with that algebra. For a conventional pair of independent subsystems, operators on either subsystem are the commutant of operators on the other, and products of the two generate the full algebra. An invertible subalgebra generalizes this idea while imposing a local reconstruction condition. The factorization need not align with the original tensor factor at each lattice site. That mismatch is essential: a chiral observable sector can have a local algebraic description without becoming a conventional on-site subsystem.

A quantum cellular automaton is an invertible transformation of operators for which both forward and inverse maps spread support only a bounded distance. A finite-depth local circuit has this property, but bounded spreading alone does not guarantee such a circuit representation. Pumping an algebraic factor between layers converts the unusual factorization into a question about information flow in one higher dimension.

**Why it matters?** The value is constructive control over a chiral sector beyond the Pauli setting. Explicit generators and local completeness let one test the relation between topological data, microscopic locality, and circuit equivalence rather than infer it only from an effective theory. This is a useful bridge between topological phases and quantum information dynamics. Its significance is primarily conceptual; turning the construction into an economical preparation or simulation protocol requires an additional implementation analysis.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 4

### Kinetic Equations and Turbulence in Large $N$ Vector and SYK-like Models
**Vladimir Rosenhaus, Daniel Schubring — arXiv:2610.19130**

A kinetic equation is more than an equation for occupation numbers: it assumes that enough microscopic information can be discarded to describe their evolution through scattering. At strong coupling, the discarded frequency structure may itself remain dynamical. Rosenhaus and Schubring use large $N$ expansions to ask which parts of kinetic theory survive far from equilibrium in two different strongly coupled model families.

For the vector models, the usual quasi-classical kinetic structure survives. General momentum-dependent interactions enter through the retarded propagator of the auxiliary collective field, which supplies the dressed scattering amplitude. Strong interaction effects can therefore be substantial without forcing a change in the basic form of the collision equation.

The large $N$ random-coupling models of Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev type behave differently. Their leading diagrams dress the individual propagators by order-one amounts and generate a finite spectral width. The resulting collision term depends on earlier-time propagators, so an instantaneous occupation distribution is insufficient to determine it. A scaling analysis gives evidence for an infrared turbulent spectrum $n_k\propto k^{-d}$, where k is momentum magnitude and d is spatial dimension. This is a candidate solution: the required convergence of the collision integral has not been established.

**Background.** The paper-specific issue is what the large-component limit organizes. In a vector model, repeated collective-channel scattering can be resummed into the auxiliary field's response. That resummation dresses the interaction while leaving a kinetic description in terms of identifiable particles or waves available. In the random-coupling family, the leading self-consistent diagrams also broaden those excitations themselves.

A narrow spectral peak allows a propagator's frequency dependence to be concentrated near an energy shell. Once the peak has appreciable width, replacing its full time dependence by an on-shell occupation and an instantaneous collision rate can lose leading information. The memory kernel retains that information. Similarly, dimensional consistency of a scale-invariant spectrum does not ensure that its collision integral is finite: scattering involving very different momenta can invalidate a nominally local cascade. This is why checking convergence is part of demonstrating turbulence, rather than a cosmetic improvement to the exponent.

**Why it matters?** The central advance is a controlled comparison between two ways strong coupling enters non-equilibrium dynamics: through a dressed scattering amplitude and through a more radical failure of time-local kinetic closure. This makes large $N$ field theory useful for identifying the assumptions behind transport descriptions, not simply for computing stronger interactions within them. Establishing convergence and dynamical formation of the proposed cascade would strengthen the more ambitious claim about a new strong-turbulence regime.

### Logical CCZ state cultivation
**Yuta Hirasaki, Jong Yeon Lee — arXiv:2610.20591**

A code can possess an attractive native logical gate without supplying a practical factory for the corresponding resource state. The gate may couple several encoded degrees of freedom in an inconvenient pattern, and a state prepared in a small block must still be moved into a more protective memory. Direct CCZ preparation is especially relevant to arithmetic, where the resource can implement Toffoli operations without first assembling them from separate T-state resources.

Hirasaki and Lee combine bivariate tricycle codes, which support a native cup-product CCZ circuit, with carefully chosen lifted bivariate bicycle codes. Selective logical initialization removes unwanted couplings so that the circuit produces two disjoint CCZ states. Decoder-based postselection rejects unfavorable syndrome histories during cultivation, and an escape stage transfers the surviving states into higher-distance memories.

Circuit-level simulations under the specified neutral-atom-motivated noise model report a joint output infidelity of $4.10\times10^{-7}$ at $8.89\%$ acceptance. The assumed single-qubit, CZ, and CCZ error rates are $0.01\%$, $0.1\%$, and $0.5\%$, respectively. The smallest factory's estimated cost per CCZ state improves on the compared route through T-state cultivation at its stated output target. Acceptance and the native three-qubit gate assumptions are integral to that resource comparison.

**Background.** The cup-product gate implements a trilinear logical phase. With several logical qubits in each participating block, its coupling pattern can involve more triples than the two desired resource states. Preparing selected logical inputs in a definite Z state suppresses the unwanted phase terms, isolating the useful triples. The issue is therefore control of the logical coupling tensor, not simply availability of a physical CCZ layer.

A projection-preserving lift enlarges the bicycle code while retaining a map to the original code's check structure. The induced logical map determines which observables can be coupled through the auxiliary block. The index-two construction is used for selective initialization; the index-three construction provides the transfer interface to the protected memory. Cultivation exploits error detection and rejection in the smaller preparation stage before that transfer. Its efficiency depends on the surviving fraction as well as on the fidelity conditional on survival.

**Why it matters?** The work turns a native algebraic operation into a more complete fault-tolerant primitive by engineering initialization, verification, and escape together. The broader design idea is to control what an inter-code map does on logical degrees of freedom, rather than treating code conversion as a generic attachment. This is directly relevant to reducing the cost of protected arithmetic, while the reported advantage should remain tied to its stated gate set, connectivity, and noise assumptions.

### Viscous hydrodynamics from correlated losses
**Aleksandr N. Mikheev, Orjan Ameye, Oded Zilberberg, Jeff Maki — arXiv:2610.19083**

A gas needs collisions that redistribute momentum before a hydrodynamic description becomes useful. Particle loss seems to work against that requirement by removing particles before they equilibrate. The distinction explored here is between independent removal and a loss process that acts only when a pair meets: the latter also modifies the scattering of particles that remain in the gas.

The authors develop a kinetic treatment of a nondegenerate three-dimensional gas with two-body loss and no ordinary elastic interaction. The optical theorem ties absorption to an elastic scattering amplitude. At strong loss, the quantum Zeno effect suppresses successful pair removal while the surviving elastic scattering approaches the unitary regime. This creates a window in which currents relax through collisions faster than the gas depletes.

The resulting viscosity and thermal conductivity approach those of a dilute unitary gas. Because loss also changes the temperature, the transport coefficients evolve during the decay, with asymptotic behavior $\eta,\kappa\propto t^{3/8}$. Here eta is viscosity, kappa is thermal conductivity, and t is time. The calculation concerns the nondegenerate regime and an intermediate-time window with appreciable density; it is not a demonstration of hydrodynamics after the gas has effectively disappeared.

**Background.** A strong absorbing channel does not simply multiply a fixed collision wavefunction by a larger loss rate. It changes that wavefunction near the region where loss occurs. In the Zeno regime, the pair's overlap with the absorbing configuration is suppressed, so increasing the nominal loss strength need not increase the actual depletion rate. Nevertheless, the outgoing wave can acquire a substantial elastic component.

That elastic component is what relaxes angular distortions of the momentum distribution. Depletion and transport therefore probe different consequences of the same two-body process, allowing their timescales to separate. The relevant meaning of the unitary limit is saturation of the scattering bound, not unitary evolution of the open gas. The temperature dependence of near-unitary transport, combined with the loss-driven heating law, then fixes the reported temporal exponent. This is why the exponent is more informative than a statement that the loss merely changes a phenomenological viscosity.

**Why it matters?** The work identifies a concrete route by which dissipation produces the collisions required for fluid behavior. It broadens the role of engineered loss from selecting states to generating transport, with a calculable time-dependent signature. For non-equilibrium physics, the useful lesson is to compare depletion with momentum relaxation rather than infer one from the other. Extending the mechanism into a quantum-degenerate regime would require new control of the distribution and scattering beyond the present calculation.

### A modular quantum computer based on Cornucopia codes
**Zhide Lu, Weikang Li, Yinghao Ma, Hanyu Yan, Bingrun Wang, Yixuan Ma, Zheng-Zhi Sun, Dong-Ling Deng — arXiv:2610.19073**

Encoding many logical qubits in one block can make a quantum memory economical while making individual logical operations difficult. A processor must address a chosen logical qubit, couple it to another, and communicate between modules without allocating a separate large ancillary apparatus to every target. The relevant resource question is whether the memory's advantage survives the workspace needed for these operations.

The authors build a processor architecture around the $[[2844,1426,18]]$ Cornucopia code. Translation symmetry organizes 1,422 of its logical qubits into cyclic registers, with four additional invariant modes. A library of seed measurement gadgets and their translates supplies individual logical addressing; matching bridges combine gadgets for joint measurements and connect separate modules.

Explicit merged-code constructions certify that the intended logical operators are measured while the code distance is preserved. For the basic instruction set, the reported average is 4.1 physical qubits per logical qubit including sufficient operation workspace, compared with 3.0 for memory alone. These counts exclude magic-state factories. Thus they quantify an important part of a processor's overhead, rather than its complete application-level cost.

**Background.** A code automorphism permutes physical qubits while preserving the stabilizer group. To turn that physical symmetry into a useful addressing rule, one must choose a compatible logical basis: translation should carry one logical coordinate to another in a predictable register. Once that basis is fixed, translating a seed gadget translates its logical target. The symmetry reduces the number of independently designed gadgets without making all logical qubits move or act identically at once.

A surgery gadget couples ancillas to a logical Pauli representative and deforms checks so that their combined outcomes reveal the target operator. Its intermediate code must avoid introducing a smaller logical error path. A matching bridge joins the fixed ports of two certified gadgets to measure a product of their targets, allowing the same interfaces to serve intra-module and inter-module operations. This is the architectural bridge from a dense memory to selectable logical measurements; a high encoding rate by itself does not provide it.

**Why it matters?** This is a concrete response to the gap between efficient storage and programmable qLDPC computation. Including operation workspace makes the comparison more meaningful than quoting memory rate alone, and reusable symmetry-related gadgets offer a plausible route to managing large logical blocks. The remaining system-level questions concern execution time, competing operations, factory provision, and achievable reliability. The paper supplies machinery with which those questions can be assessed rather than settling them through the encoding rate.

### Quantum Folding in Chebyshev-Based Quantum Subspace Diagonalization
**Seung Park, Dongni Chen, Stefano Chesi, Kyunghyun Baek, Mahn-Soo Choi — arXiv:2610.19922**

A ground-state algorithm can converge rapidly inside a chosen subspace yet remain expensive because its reference state barely overlaps the desired state. That overlap also affects sensitivity to measurement noise and the probability of preparing the state represented by a classical eigenvector. Improving the subspace solver alone therefore leaves a common initialization bottleneck unresolved.

The authors propose quantum folding: solve smaller subsystems first and use the product of their ground states as the reference for the joined system. For a local Hamiltonian, this reference already incorporates correlations within each subsystem; the omitted couplings lie at their interfaces. They combine it with a Chebyshev–Krylov subspace method, establish an error bound linear in measurement noise, and make the Hamiltonian's block-encoding normalization explicit in the guarantee.

Spin-chain calculations show sizable reductions in subspace dimension and noise error relative to a simple reference. The analysis also follows the cost of converting the Ritz vector into a physical state. Fold overlaps approach nonzero constants in the studied gapped examples, while the gapless chain has a slow power-law decrease. Recursive costs depend on these overlaps and the filter degree; the gapless analysis contains a mild quasi-polynomial overlap factor, so the broad scalability claim should be read with the stated model and asymptotic assumptions.

**Background.** A Krylov method filters a reference state by applying successive polynomials of the Hamiltonian and then solves an effective eigenproblem in their span. A Chebyshev basis provides useful approximation control after rescaling the spectrum into a bounded interval. However, a small computed energy error is different from a large probability of successfully preparing the corresponding physical state. A filter still has to amplify the desired component relative to the reference's initial overlap.

Folding changes where that overlap is lost. Joining two correlated pieces removes an artificial boundary instead of rebuilding correlations throughout both pieces from an uncorrelated product. Whether this is economical depends on the response to the interface, including gaplessness and edge sectors. Furthermore, the rescaling used by a block encoding changes the dimensionless spectral gap seen by the polynomial filter. A favorable subspace dimension therefore cannot be equated directly with shallow circuits.

**Why it matters?** The contribution connects reference-state design, noisy energy estimation, and actual state preparation within one analysis. It provides a physically motivated alternative to treating initialization overlap as a fixed external parameter. For quantum simulation, that is a useful methodological advance: it identifies both a way to improve the input and the assumptions needed for recursive gains. Extensions should be judged by interface overlaps and full preparation costs, rather than by energy convergence alone.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 9

### Petz Recovery through a Single Polar Transformation for Approximate Quantum Error Correction
**Hanzhang Zhao, Shenglong Xu — arXiv:2610.20515**

The Petz map gives a broadly useful recovery prescription, but a compact operator formula is not yet an efficient decoder circuit. Its inverse square-root normalization can be costly to approximate, especially near small singular values. Zhao and Xu reorganize the map so that the recovery transformation can be implemented as one singular-value operation rather than several separately approximated matrix functions.

They expose the polar structure of the Petz recovery isometry, give explicit block-encoding circuits, and construct an extended isometry that implements approximate recovery without postselection. A spectrum-weighted error measure bounds the distance between the approximate and ideal encoding-recovery channels, providing an objective for choosing the quantum singular value transformation angles for a particular noise channel.

Uniform polynomial approximation gives exponentially decreasing error with iteration count, at a rate controlled by the smallest nonzero scaled singular value. Applications to random encodings and the Hayden–Preskill setting illustrate the tradeoff between depth and recovery accuracy. The absence of postselection removes a probabilistic acceptance bottleneck; it does not remove the spectral dependence or the cost of realizing the required encoding and channel access.

**Background.** In a polar decomposition, an operator is separated into a positive magnitude and a partial isometry. Schematically, $A=V\sqrt{A^\dagger A}$. If the desired recovery isometry is identified with the polar factor V of a suitable stacked channel operator, its construction becomes a transformation of singular values while retaining their left and right singular vectors.

A block encoding places a rescaled nonunitary operator in a designated block of a larger unitary. Quantum singular value transformation then uses a sequence of controlled rotations and that unitary to approximate a chosen function on its singular spectrum. Approximating a sign-like polar transformation near zero is costly, which explains the dependence on the smallest supported singular value. A spectrum-weighted objective can instead prioritize the singular modes that actually contribute to the channel's recovery performance. This distinguishes a useful channel-specific circuit from a uniformly accurate approximation over an unnecessarily large interval.

**Why it matters?** The work gives an implementation-oriented bridge between approximate QEC recovery theory and quantum algorithms for matrix functions. Combining the normalization and reversal steps through one polar structure can simplify the circuit design and its error accounting. The many-body examples also make it useful for comparing scrambling decoders on common terms. Its practical advantage will depend on the access model and spectrum of the particular code and noise channel, which the formulation makes explicit.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 10

### Droplets obstruct Onsager-type solutions of the Ising model in a magnetic field
**Ran Luo, Jiahua Tian — arXiv:2610.20569**

The absence of an Onsager-like solution for the Ising model in a field has long invited a question about what kind of obstruction is involved. Is a suitable closed expression merely undiscovered, or do the thermodynamic contributions already rule out a broad class of candidate formulas? This paper turns droplet geometry into such an obstruction, with a precise statement about the coupling dependence of the free energy.

For dimensions $d\geq2$ and every fixed dimensionless field outside a countable exceptional set, the authors show that the free energy obeys no algebraic differential equation in the coupling, even locally. The proof uses the growth of the largest droplet volume at fixed boundary size and an integer-band structure in the expansion coefficients that prevents the decisive contributions from canceling. This excludes Onsager-type representations and the stated classes of elliptic, hypergeometric, and Painlevé expressions in the coupling.

The result does not show that all exact descriptions are impossible. Difference equations and other representations remain outside the excluded class. Nor does the exceptional set identify a collection of fields at which an exact solution is available: it marks the scope of the theorem.

**Background.** An algebraic differential equation is a polynomial relation involving a function, a finite number of its derivatives, and the independent variable. Differential transcendence rules out every such finite relation. It is much stronger than failure to express a function in elementary functions, but it is still a restriction on a particular mathematical class of descriptions.

The distinction between surface and volume is the physical input. In a low-temperature expansion, the boundary counts broken interaction bonds while the interior controls the magnetic-field cost. In dimensions above one, compact droplets enclose a volume growing faster than their boundary size. The proof extracts an extremal part of that geometry whose coefficient cannot disappear through cancellation with other droplets. The variables held fixed matter: the theorem varies $K=J/(k_BT)$ at fixed $H=h/(k_BT)$, with J the interaction, h the field energy, and T temperature. This is a statement about dimensionless parameter space, not an unrestricted assertion about every path through physical field and temperature.

**Why it matters?** The advance is a concrete boundary on the forms an exact solution can take, tied to the geometry that makes finite-dimensional lattice systems different from simpler approximations. It sharpens an integrability question without confusing one excluded solution class with all possible solvability. For statistical mechanics, it is especially useful as an example of microscopic excitations constraining the analytic structure of a thermodynamic function, rather than merely producing nonanalytic behavior at a phase transition.

### Mutual Information of Measurement in Conformal Field Theories
**Israel Klich, Ravisankar Rajagopal, Diana Vaman — arXiv:2610.19286**

Choosing a local observable turns a quantum field state into a distribution of measurement outcomes. The resulting spatial information need not track entanglement in a simple way: the observable and the order in which measurement and spatial partitioning are performed both matter. The authors ask which universal critical features survive in the mutual information of these outcome distributions.

For scalar primaries and descendants in a large $N$ conformal regime, they evaluate the measurement information for adjacent and widely separated intervals. Gaussian functional determinants reduce the calculation to restricted two-point kernels. In the adjacent limit, the logarithmic coefficient is proportional to $(\Delta-\tfrac12)^2$, where delta is the measured operator's scaling dimension. Derivatives can therefore increase that coefficient, apparently conflicting with the expectation that processing should reduce mutual information.

The resolution is operational. A derivative formed across a shared boundary uses data from both sides. Measuring that derivative field and then assigning its outcomes to regions is different from processing each region's original outcomes independently. The result therefore does not violate local data processing. The universal continuum expressions also require attention to regulator and contact terms, and should not be promoted to general finite $N$ interacting-CFT formulas.

**Background.** The specialized step is the determinant reduction. At Gaussian order, a measured field configuration is characterized by its covariance kernel. Restricting that kernel to a region and combining the resulting determinants isolates the shared information. One can consequently obtain nontrivial spatial information from conformal two-point data without solving a full probability distribution with independent higher connected correlators.

The boundary issue is visible on a lattice: a difference between two neighboring field values crosses the partition if those neighbors lie in different regions. Calling that difference a local continuum operator does not make the corresponding operation local with respect to the chosen bipartition. The two descriptions use different meanings of locality. Similarly, contact terms encode the short-distance measurement prescription. Removing or retaining them changes what continuum quantity is being defined, so a conformally organized answer still needs a specified relation to regulated measurements.

**Why it matters?** This gives controlled benchmarks for how observable choice shapes information extracted from critical matter. The operational clarification is useful for quantum simulation and monitored systems, where measured or processed outcomes often stand in for properties of the underlying state. Its conceptual value is the distinction between information lost through separate regional processing and information imported by a boundary-crossing observable. Extending the calculation beyond Gaussian order would test how much of the universality survives genuinely interacting measurement statistics.

## arXiv-2026-10-15 — Item 12

### Optimal Disorder Strength for Approximate Unitary $k$-Designs in Spin Chains
**Tian-Gang Zhou, Yi-Neng Zhou, Thierry Giamarchi, Julian Sonner — arXiv:2610.18954**

Randomized measurement protocols need an ensemble with the right moments, not merely a Hamiltonian whose spectrum looks chaotic. Disordered analog dynamics offers a natural source of random transformations, but the tuning problem is competitive: weak disorder supplies little variation, while strong disorder inhibits the transitions needed to explore the Hilbert space. An intermediate optimum is plausible, yet a useful design principle should explain both sides of that competition.

The authors analyze the moment operator of a disordered interacting XXZ chain within a fixed-magnetization sector. Perturbative estimates show how the deviation from the Haar moment operator decreases with disorder on the weak side and returns toward a poorly mixing regime on the strong side. Their crossover places the useful disorder scale near the transverse exchange energy. Independent successive quenches further suppress the moment error exponentially in the number of segments.

Numerical calculations support these trends and demonstrate a classical-shadow application. The target ensemble covers the specified symmetry sector, not arbitrary unitaries on the entire spin Hilbert space. The estimates also concern controlled asymptotic regimes and finite-size comparisons; they do not establish uniformly efficient generation of every higher design order in large systems.

**Background.** The paper's moment gap is the operator-norm deviation from the Haar moment operator. It measures the slowest remaining error direction in the ensemble average, rather than an excitation energy. This is more directly tied to the required randomized protocol than an energy-level-spacing diagnostic, because moment accuracy is a statement about the transformations themselves.

With successive independent disorder draws, the moment operators compose. On the subspace orthogonal to the Haar-invariant component, each successful mixing segment acts as a contraction. Submultiplicativity then explains the exponential improvement under repeated quenches: a residual deviation smaller than one is multiplied repeatedly. Reusing one static Hamiltonian is not the same operation, because the successive segments would no longer be independent ensemble draws. On the strong-disorder side, large longitudinal fields suppress transverse transitions, leaving a basis-locked ensemble that changing phases alone cannot make Haar-like.

**Why it matters?** This turns a qualitative competition between randomness and localization into a protocol-oriented tuning argument. It links a microscopic energy scale to the quality of a transformation ensemble and explains why refreshing the disorder can matter as much as increasing evolution time. For quantum simulation and measurement algorithms, the contribution is a useful bridge between many-body dynamics and the particular moments a task requires. Assessing larger systems should retain that task-specific criterion rather than substitute a general chaos indicator.
