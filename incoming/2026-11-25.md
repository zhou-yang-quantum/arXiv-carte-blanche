## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 1

### Spinal reflected entropy reveals logical magic
**Zong-Yue Hou, Yuzhen Zhang, Zhi-Cheng Yang, Yingfei Gu — arXiv:2611.31375**

Can spatial entanglement reveal the nonstabilizer resource stored in a topological quantum memory? Ordinary disk entropies cannot distinguish logical states, while a cut around one handle probes only a particular logical basis. Extracting magic requires access to the full set of logical Pauli expectations.

The authors construct a partition following a spine of the surface: noncontractible loops meeting at one vertex. After removing a correctable disk, they evaluate reflected entropy between a neighborhood of the vertex and bands following the loops. Beyond the boundary and topological contributions, the entropy contains a logical term satisfying

$$\zeta_n(\psi)=M_n(\psi)+\log\mathrm{GSD}.$$

Here the right side contains the stored state's stabilizer Rényi entropy and the logarithm of the ground-state degeneracy. The identity follows because doubling the punctured surface turns the bands into tubes whose anyon charges have the probability distribution associated with Bell sampling. The construction extends to higher genus and, under the stated topological assumptions, two-dimensional qubit stabilizer codes.

**Background.** Reflected entropy replaces a mixed density matrix by its canonical purification on an original and conjugate system, then measures entanglement across the doubled partition. In this code setting, the reduced density matrix is proportional to a projector, so vectorizing it gives that purification. The tube-charge probabilities are normalized squared logical Pauli expectations. Their Rényi entropy includes the stabilizer-state baseline; subtracting that baseline gives the magic measure. Doubling therefore makes information from intersecting logical cycles available within one entanglement calculation.

**Why it matters?** This is a conceptual connection between two resources usually defined using different structures. It gives logical magic a geometric realization within topological order and makes entanglement methods relevant to encoded computational resources. Extending such a correspondence to noisy logical states or more general topological phases would require additional arguments; the present result establishes a clean foundation in the stabilizer setting.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 2

### A spin chain as a deformed conformal field theory: an ODE/IM approach
**Sujay K. Ashok, Nicolò Primi, Roberto Tateo — arXiv:2611.31417**

A conformal field theory captures the leading low-energy spectrum of a critical spin chain, but finite chains retain an infinite hierarchy of lattice corrections. Describing those corrections systematically requires identifying how irrelevant interactions modify both the energy and the higher conserved charges.

For a specially twisted XXZ chain in its Lee–Yang scaling sector, the authors reconstruct these corrections using the ODE/IM correspondence. Each edge of the Bethe-root distribution becomes a chiral generalized Gibbs ensemble, represented by a deformed cubic oscillator. Finite size couples the two edges: conserved charges from one chirality shift the effective chemical potentials of the other. This feedback has a generalized $T\bar{T}$ structure.

Solving the feedback self-consistently produces parameter-free expansions in $1/N$ for the energy and local charges. Comparisons with Bethe-ansatz results cover the ground state and an asymmetric excited state. The construction concerns this special chain and scaling sector; its broader applicability remains a question.

**Background.** The ODE/IM correspondence identifies spectral information of an ordinary differential equation with conserved quantities of an integrable quantum theory. Stokes multipliers describe how solutions decaying in different complex-plane directions connect; their asymptotic expansion encodes the quantum charges. A generalized Gibbs ensemble assigns separate chemical potentials to the commuting charges. Here those potentials organize lattice corrections, rather than describe ordinary thermal equilibrium. The important extra step is coupling the two chiral reconstructions, so each oscillator depends on charges calculated from its partner.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a constructive bridge between a microscopic lattice spectrum and an irrelevant deformation of its continuum theory. Its appeal is the common mechanism controlling energy and higher charges, which makes it more informative than fitting isolated finite-size coefficients. It is directly relevant to understanding how integrability and $T\bar{T}$-type deformations organize the approach to a continuum limit.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 3

### Locality from Nonlocality: Symmetry Relocalization and Gauging of Lattice Dirac Fermions
**Lei Su — arXiv:2611.31366**

A continuum symmetry can have an exact lattice ancestor whose generator is spatially nonlocal. Whether that symmetry admits local gauge dynamics then becomes a separate question: making a charge look local can introduce long-range hopping into the Hamiltonian.

The paper develops symmetry relocalization, using nonlocal canonical transformations to make selected compact charges onsite while retaining enough Hamiltonian locality for gauging. Its clearest example is a half-filled fermion chain. A particle–hole transformation exchanges its onsite vector charge with a nonlocal spectral axial charge while preserving the hopping Hamiltonian. Each symmetry can consequently be gauged in an appropriate description, with their mixed anomaly retained.

Higher-dimensional constructions establish model-dependent forms of exact or emergent gauge locality. The analysis also identifies obstructions, including a restricted no-go theorem for a Weyl doublet. These results control symmetry realization and specified gauge regulators; they do not determine the eventual interacting infrared theory.

**Background.** The spectral axial charge assigns opposite integer charges to momentum modes near the two Fermi points. The integer spectrum makes the symmetry compact, but the sharp momentum assignment gives its real-space kernel long tails. Relocalization changes the microscopic fermion coordinates so this charge becomes ordinary particle number. Hamiltonian locality must then be checked independently. In the one-dimensional example, nonlocality moves to the other charge, preserving the obstruction to making both symmetries onsite simultaneously.

**Why it matters?** The work sharpens the distinction between an exact symmetry, a local gauge regulator, and a desired interacting continuum limit. That distinction matters for lattice QFT and quantum simulation, where a low-energy symmetry alone provides insufficient control over dynamical gauge fields. The explicit constructions offer useful tools, while the anomaly constraints identify where a change of variables cannot remove the underlying obstruction.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 4

### Classical algorithms for sparse random Hamiltonians via free probability
**Matthew Ding, Robbie King, Thomas Schuster — arXiv:2611.33128**

Rapid entanglement growth and quantum-chaotic behavior make random many-body Hamiltonians attractive candidates for quantum advantage. Yet reproducing particular observables may require much less information than representing the evolving state. The difficult test is whether a classical method can recover instance-specific fluctuations beyond ensemble averages.

The authors construct such a method for the sparse Pauli model. They compute a short hierarchy of free cumulants, which organize deviations from random-matrix behavior, and resum their contributions to spectral, thermal, and dynamical observables. This includes out-of-time-order correlators and a model with a spectral transition.

The rigorous regime is explicit. For $n$ qubits and $m$ random Pauli terms, the main theorem assumes $m=\Omega(n^3)$, times and inverse temperatures $t,\beta=o(n/\log n)$, and precision $\varepsilon=2^{-o(n/\log n)}$. The precision dependence is $(1/\varepsilon)^{2+o(1)}$, with further polynomial factors. The comparison still permits an amplitude-estimation advantage and leaves more extreme regimes open.

**Background.** Free probability organizes noncommuting random variables using noncrossing partitions. Its free cumulants play a role analogous to connected cumulants in ordinary probability. The semicircle distribution supplies the leading behavior; higher cumulants encode corrections specific to the Hamiltonian. Their concentration makes a truncated hierarchy effective even when direct state evolution becomes difficult. Resumming those few corrections recovers information that simply replacing the spectrum by its average would lose.

**Why it matters?** This is a substantial classical benchmark for proposed quantum simulations of chaotic systems. It shows why difficult state representations and conspicuous scrambling signatures do not, by themselves, establish difficult observable prediction. For quantum dynamics and algorithm design, the useful lesson is to identify which physical structure survives the averaging and which observables actually evade economical resummation.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 5

### Bound-state preparation in strongly coupled theories
**Scott Lawrence, Duff Neill — arXiv:2611.33079**

Scattering simulations require isolated incoming particles, often composite bound states. Preparing them by slowly turning on interactions can fail when no suitable path from a free theory is known. The authors instead borrow a physical production mechanism: create an easily controlled particle and let it decay into the desired strongly coupled excitation.

Weakly coupled auxiliary fields provide a parent and a recoil particle, giving a decay channel $\zeta\to\Psi+\nu$. The coupling operator selects the target's quantum numbers, while the auxiliary masses restrict accessible final states. Measuring the auxiliaries heralds decay and helps specify the target's motion. Only a small part of their Hilbert spaces is needed, allowing economical encodings.

Classical simulations demonstrate the procedure in one-dimensional nonrelativistic models, including a bound state. A spectral-function model estimates charmonium production. Long evolution and weak coupling improve selectivity, while faster production increases contamination. The method assumes an available interacting vacuum and suitable production operator; efficient preparation is not established for arbitrary bound states.

**Background.** The recoil field supplies a continuum of final kinetic energies, allowing genuine decay while conserving total energy and momentum. Choosing a production threshold below unwanted excitations helps isolate the target, but a finite-duration pulse broadens energy resolution. Consequently, observing parent disappearance alone does not certify a pure target eigenstate. Weisskopf–Wigner analysis estimates the survival amplitude from the spectral density seen by the production operator, making the yield predictable when that density is sufficiently known.

**Why it matters?** This is a physically motivated addition to the state-preparation toolbox for strongly coupled QFT. It can produce useful overlap without first solving the bound-state wavefunction or using phase estimation. The numerical evidence is limited, but the mechanism addresses a central scattering bottleneck and exposes concrete questions about spectral selectivity, ultraviolet excitation, and the preparation-time cost.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 6

### Exact nonstationary heat-excess statistics in the weakly asymmetric Kipnis--Marchioro--Presutti model
**Baruch Meerson — arXiv:2611.31303**

The average spreading of a heat pulse conceals the rare histories that move an unusually large fraction of its energy to one side. Finding their probabilities requires solving a nonlinear fluctuation problem with conditions imposed at both the initial and final times. Integrability of the evolution equations alone does not solve that boundary-value problem.

Meerson finds a nonlocal canonical transformation that removes the drive from the weakly asymmetric KMP model while preserving the fluctuation action and both temporal conditions. It maps the problem to its already solved symmetric counterpart. The resulting rate function is

$$s(j;\epsilon)=s_0\!\left(\frac{e^{2\epsilon j}-\cosh\epsilon}{2\sinh\epsilon}\right).$$

Here $j$ is the heat excess relative to an equal split, divided by the pulse energy; $\epsilon$ combines the bias and total energy; and $s_0$ is the symmetric rate function. The result covers the full allowed excess range in the leading long-time hydrodynamic regime and extends to an arbitrary observation point.

**Background.** KMP dynamics randomly redistributes neighboring sites' combined nonnegative energy. Macroscopic fluctuation theory describes a rare transfer through an optimal density history and a conjugate field imposing the final constraint. The transformation uses cumulative heat, so changing variables at one position depends on energy elsewhere. Its crucial property is preservation of the action: the driven and symmetric histories have the same fluctuation cost after a nonlinear remapping of the measured excess.

**Why it matters?** This is an exact solution of a genuinely time-dependent interacting fluctuation problem, including rare tails beyond Gaussian response. The methodological advance is especially useful: it identifies when a change of variables transports the entire constrained problem, rather than only its bulk equations. That offers a productive route to further solvable nonequilibrium models.

### Hadron structure in $1+1$ dimensions from the infinite Hamiltonian lattice
**Ross Dempsey — arXiv:2611.31750**

A bound-state mass reveals little about how momentum and energy are distributed among its constituents. Those questions require matrix elements between momentum eigenstates, including lightlike correlations that are awkward in Euclidean simulations. This paper develops their calculation directly on an infinite Hamiltonian lattice.

In the massive Schwinger model, a uniform matrix product state represents the vacuum, and a momentum-resolved quasiparticle ansatz represents mesons. The method computes parton distribution functions and gravitational form factors, benchmarking against discretized lightcone quantization at $\theta=0$ before exploring nonzero theta.

The resulting structure functions resolve qualitative changes near $\theta=\pi$. Above the critical fermion mass, approaching this angle produces large, loosely bound hadrons. Approaching the Ising critical point from below along that angle changes the gravitational form factor toward the free-Majorana form. The numerical construction targets stable states below the relevant multiparticle threshold.

**Background.** The quasiparticle ansatz replaces one vacuum tensor by an excitation tensor and sums its position with a plane-wave phase. This yields definite momentum directly in infinite volume. Gauge charge labels on the virtual bonds implement Gauss's law. Parton distribution functions probe constituent longitudinal momentum, whereas gravitational form factors come from stress-tensor matrix elements and characterize energy and momentum structure. The theta angle acts as a background electric field, changing the force binding the charged constituents.

**Why it matters?** The contribution combines a reusable matrix-element framework with a physical account of how confinement and criticality reshape composite particles. Infinite volume is particularly valuable when the bound-state size grows. Among the batch's several Schwinger-model structure papers, this one stands out for connecting hadron observables to the crossover into an emergent critical field theory.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 8

### Coherent meson-baryon conversion in a cold-atom non-Abelian lattice gauge simulator
**Tian-Yi Wang, Wei-Yong Zhang, Tie-Jin Zhong, Ming-Gen He, Zhao-Yu Zhou, Hui Sun, Zhen-Sheng Yuan, Jian-Wei Pan — arXiv:2611.31888**

Real-time conversion between different composite particles is a natural target for quantum simulation of gauge theories. Engineering non-Abelian gauge–matter dynamics remains difficult, particularly when both the gauge field and matter must evolve coherently.

The experiment encodes a sector of a one-dimensional SU(2) quantum link model using two internal states and occupation configurations of rubidium atoms. Resonant tunneling implements the allowed gauge–matter transitions, with independently tunable mass and gauge coupling. Site-resolved measurements follow slow conversion ramps and coherent oscillations between mesonic configurations and baryon–antibaryon pairs.

The scope of the encoding is essential. Gauge links retain only the spin-zero and spin-one-half representations, and the simulated sector contains static adjoint background charges. Within that sector, the authors compare hadron populations and dynamics with numerical simulations and vary energy and net baryon density.

**Background.** A quantum link model replaces each gauge link's infinite-dimensional rotor space by a finite representation space. Gauge constraints then restrict which matter and link configurations can coexist. In SU(2), two quarks can form a color singlet, so baryons contain two constituents; mesons combine a quark and antiquark. Atomic tunneling connects occupation patterns representing these different allowed composites. Coherent conversion tests their coupling within the constrained gauge Hilbert space, beyond merely preparing isolated configurations.

**Why it matters?** This experiment earns inclusion because it demonstrates a substantive theoretical principle: coherent interconversion of gauge-constrained composites with dynamical matter and flux. It establishes a platform for asking how reversible microscopic reactions lead to chemical equilibration. Connecting that capability to less truncated sectors, higher dimensions, or QCD requires substantial further development.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 9

### Neural Decoding of Logical Clifford Circuits with Transversal Gates
**Yicheng Guang, Yuliang Xiong, Xun Gao, Yueqi Chen — arXiv:2611.31607**

Fast transversal logical gates spread errors between code blocks, making independent block decoding inadequate. A neural decoder can learn these correlations, but part of its task is already fixed exactly by Clifford propagation. Duet incorporates that known structure into the network.

Features are exchanged only between locations connected by the physical gates. Separate outputs predict each layer's contribution to final logical flips, then combine those contributions through a soft XOR. Shared network weights accommodate different block counts and circuit depths at a fixed code distance. A confidence rule sends difficult shots to the Tesseract search decoder.

In simulated surface-code Clifford circuits, the combination substantially improves accuracy over the reported baselines, including a factor of 44 fewer failures than LoMatching at distance nine in one benchmark. Its speed results use batched GPU inference and CPU search. The evidence concerns specified stochastic noise models and circuit families.

**Background.** A soft XOR combines probabilistic predictions according to parity, matching how Pauli errors alter a final logical observable. Backward propagation identifies which logical observable each layer must predict. The circuits also include a fold-transversal S gate: mirror-related qubits across a patch diagonal are coupled by CZ operations within the syndrome round. Duet routes information along these within-block connections as well as between blocks, so the architecture follows the actual error propagation geometry.

**Why it matters?** The useful advance is a principled division between exact circuit algebra and learned error inference. This could make neural decoding more transferable across logical circuits while reserving expensive search for ambiguous syndromes. Larger computations and noise with coherent components remain important tests, but the present benchmarks provide a concrete accuracy–throughput improvement for transversal architectures.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 10

### When Randomness Hides Quantum Simulation Errors
**Yue Cao, Tianfeng Feng, Jonathan Allcock, Chenfeng Cao, Qi Zhao — arXiv:2611.32497**

A Trotterized simulator can reproduce measured observables surprisingly well despite substantial worst-case evolution error. Assessing that success requires understanding the input ensemble: random states can suppress the visibility of errors without improving the implemented propagator.

The paper studies the Heisenberg-picture error observable $B(M)=U_0^\dagger M U_0-U^\dagger M U$, comparing ideal and implemented evolution of the measured observable. Mean absolute error bounds for state 2-designs can be exponentially smaller in qubit number than tight bounds for state 1-designs. The excess second frame potential controls departure from the Haar second moment, while its orientation relative to accessible observables determines which errors remain visible.

For an order-$p$ formula over time $T$, the authors also obtain a sufficient step count $\mathcal O(T^{1+3/(4p)})$ under a specified pre-saturation decay of the temporal frame potential. This is a conditional, averaged guarantee.

**Background.** The second frame potential averages fourth powers of pairwise state overlaps. Its excess above the Haar value quantifies how much second-moment structure remains in the ensemble. The paper then resolves that structure into observable directions: two ensembles with comparable total deviation can expose different errors if their residual moments align differently with the error map. This explains why choosing measurements matters alongside increasing randomness.

**Why it matters?** The result connects equilibration-style state statistics to practical simulation validation and resource estimates. It supports shallower circuits when the relevant average error is controlled, while showing why good performance on randomized inputs may transfer poorly to structured physical states. The most useful application is to design benchmarks whose states and observables expose the errors relevant to the intended dynamics.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 11

### Anomalous front propagation in many-body quantum transport with memory-dependent resetting
**Gabriele de Mauro, Manas Kulkarni, Satya N. Majumdar — arXiv:2611.32961**

Returning a spreading quantum system repeatedly to a fixed state commonly arrests transport. What happens when each reset instead selects a state from the system's own history? The expanding archive of past states allows continued spreading, while introducing memory into the dynamics.

The authors derive an exact representation of this protocol for any time-independent Hamiltonian. The ensemble density matrix is an average of reset-free evolution over a random effective time. They apply it to domain-wall melting in a free-fermion chain and obtain density profiles, front-edge statistics, and integrated-current fluctuations.

Memory changes ballistic propagation to a front position growing as $\ln t$. The macroscopic profile retains its scaling function, but the edge width grows as $\sqrt{\ln t}$ rather than $t^{1/3}$, and the rightmost-particle statistics become Gaussian instead of Tracy–Widom. The general representation allows interactions; the explicit transport results concern the free chain.

**Background.** At Poisson-distributed reset events, the entire many-body state returns to one visited at a uniformly sampled earlier physical time. Because every trajectory remains on the same unitary orbit, a single random clock parametrizes its quantum state. That clock advances only logarithmically at late times and has growing fluctuations shared by all particles. Averaging over it preserves broad profile features while reorganizing collective edge fluctuations. This is a prescribed feedback protocol rather than a microscopic bath derivation.

**Why it matters?** This provides an unusually transparent solvable model of memory altering many-body transport and fluctuation statistics. Its separation of unitary physics from a stochastic clock offers a reusable calculation method. Applying it to interacting reset-free dynamics would test which transport signatures survive that averaging and which are replaced by fluctuations of the collective clock.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 12

### Space-Time Point Defects as Probes of Criticality: Application to the Gross-Neveu-Heisenberg Transition
**Jakob Väth, Luis Thremer, Francesco Parisen Toldin, Fakher F. Assaad — arXiv:2611.32775**

A longstanding discrepancy in a fermionic critical theory may partly reflect how its correlations are measured. Field-theory estimates of the order-parameter anomalous dimension near one have differed from numerical estimates extracted from equal-time correlations.

The authors insert a source at one point in Euclidean space-time and measure the induced one-point profile. It encodes the desired two-point function while providing a better-behaved estimator in auxiliary-field quantum Monte Carlo. Applied to the two-flavor Gross–Neveu–Heisenberg transition of a lattice Dirac model, the method yields $\nu=1.10(3)$ and $\eta_\phi=1.09(4)$.

The finding $\eta_\phi>1$ explains a key difficulty: the equal-time structure factor has a nondivergent critical contribution and is dominated by background terms. The authors instead extract scaling from the space-time susceptibility, with finite-size corrections included in their analysis.

**Background.** Gross–Neveu–Heisenberg criticality couples gapless Dirac fermions to a three-component magnetic order parameter, allowing spontaneous fermion mass generation. A source localized at one Euclidean point is an irrelevant perturbation, so its distant response retains bulk critical scaling. A static pinning field extends throughout imaginary time and can generate a separate defect critical problem. Here absorbing one operator insertion into the statistical weight also avoids products of Green's functions responsible for rare, very large estimator fluctuations.

**Why it matters?** The method offers both a practical estimator improvement and a plausible resolution of an important field-theory/numerics disagreement. More broadly, it illustrates how a critical exponent can make a familiar diagnostic insensitive to the singular physics. The numerical estimate remains subject to finite-size analysis, but the estimator construction should be useful beyond this particular universality class.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 13

### Differential Equations for Feynman Integrals from Higher Residue Pairings
**Giacomo Brunello, Vsevolod Chestnov, Ben Page — arXiv:2611.32544**

Differentiating a multiloop integral with respect to external kinematics generates other integrals that must be reduced to a finite basis. Integration-by-parts reduction can become the dominant computational obstacle. This paper constructs the differential system directly through intersection pairings.

In the Lee–Pomeransky representation, the relevant pairings localize at critical points of a potential. Their formal large-$\varepsilon$ expansion, with $\varepsilon$ the dimensional regulator, is organized by higher residue pairings. For systems at most linear in that regulator, the first two orders determine the differential equations exactly.

The main technical step is removing an auxiliary analytic regulator. Under assumptions on isolated, nondegenerate critical points and their analytic dependence, the limiting points organize into sectors corresponding to pinched propagators. Explicit sectorwise formulas produce canonical differential equations for massless four-point ladders through four loops, including a new four-loop system.

**Background.** An intersection pairing supplies projection coefficients onto a basis of integrands modulo total derivatives. It plays the role of pairing vectors with a dual basis, without evaluating the Feynman integrals themselves. Higher residue pairings express this projection through local derivatives near critical points. The large-regulator expansion is an algebraic reconstruction tool: the rational dependence of the answer allows finitely many coefficients to fix the required system. Pinched propagators identify lower-topology sectors that must remain present when the auxiliary regulator disappears.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful methodological advance for perturbative QFT, replacing a potentially large reduction problem with multivariate local data and a sector hierarchy. The four-loop application demonstrates meaningful reach. Establishing comparable efficiency for more scales, masses, and systems with more complicated regulator dependence would determine how broadly it can complement existing reduction methods.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 14

### Relaxation dynamics of the impurity momentum in a one-dimensional Fermi gas
**Felipe Taha Sant'Ana — arXiv:2611.32280**

An impurity injected into an integrable gas can retain part of its initial momentum and exhibit long-lived oscillations. Exact spectra alone do not determine that relaxation: one also needs off-diagonal matrix elements connecting the populated eigenstates.

For an equal-mass impurity repulsively coupled to free spinless fermions, the author derives closed determinant expressions for the impurity momentum and its square between Bethe-ansatz eigenstates. They make the post-injection spectral sum explicit, resolve quantum flutter, and connect finite-system calculations to Fredholm determinants in the thermodynamic limit.

At infinite repulsion, a selection rule removes matrix elements between distinct states sharing the same rapidity, and every eigenstate carries the same per-particle share of total momentum. Consequently,

$$P_\infty=\frac{p_0}{N+1}\longrightarrow0.$$

Here $N$ counts host fermions and $p_0$ is the fixed injection momentum. At finite coupling, a nonzero stationary momentum can remain. The infinite-coupling statement concerns the mean; the injection protocol has singular high-momentum behavior in higher moments.

**Background.** Quantum flutter denotes oscillatory momentum exchange during impurity relaxation. Its calculation requires interference between interacting eigenstates, so determinant form factors provide information unavailable from energies alone. The diagonal ensemble retains the initial overlap weights after dephasing. Degenerate states could also contribute to a time average; the paper's matrix-element identities control those contributions and justify the stationary-momentum formula.

**Why it matters?** The main contribution is a reusable exact dynamical observable in a benchmark interacting integrable model. The infinite-coupling result also clarifies that integrability does not force every observable to retain a finite memory of its initial value. This makes the model useful for separating conserved information, dephasing, and singular strong-coupling limits in nonequilibrium dynamics.

## arXiv-2026-11-25 — Item 15

### Thermal state preparation with shallow quantum circuits
**Cenk Tüysüz, Simon Deconihout, Enrico Rinaldi, Michele Grossi — arXiv:2611.32388**

Finite-temperature dynamical simulations must spend part of their circuit budget preparing the initial thermal state. Even when a classical representation is available, converting it into a hardware circuit can consume too much depth. This paper designs the preparation circuit directly within a shallow architecture.

PP-VarQITE combines variational imaginary-time evolution with Pauli propagation, computing circuit parameters entirely classically. Temperatures are encoded in different angle sets for a fixed architecture. A shared representation of circuit derivatives keeps the variational metric positive semidefinite even after the operator expansions are truncated.

Numerical tests cover Ising and frustrated Heisenberg models. Hardware demonstrations prepare a 48-site frustrated chain and a seven-by-seven Ising lattice using 96 and 98 qubits, respectively, resolving dimer order and extended correlations. Depth improves over the tested tensor-network compilation routes, although variational trajectories can show delayed cooling and smaller depth need not imply smaller observable error.

**Background.** Thermal purification represents a mixed Gibbs state as part of a pure state with an auxiliary register. Starting from physical–auxiliary Bell pairs gives infinite temperature; imaginary-time evolution on the physical register generates the target purification. Pauli propagation evaluates the needed circuit quantities by moving operators backward through gates. Branching expansions are truncated to keep the classical computation manageable. Reusing the same approximate derivative vectors builds a consistent Gram matrix, avoiding an unphysical metric caused by independently approximating its entries.

**Why it matters?** This is a practical compilation advance with hardware evidence for correlated thermal states. Its broader value is the possibility of reserving more circuit depth for subsequent real-time measurements. The preparation is approximate and classically designed, so the useful next test is whether its observable accuracy remains sufficient for dynamical quantities that are difficult to calculate classically.
