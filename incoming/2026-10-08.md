## arXiv-2026-10-08 — Item 1

### A gauge-invariant measure for lattice chiral fermions
**Nathaniel Craig — arXiv:2610.09093**

A nonperturbative formulation of a chiral gauge theory must preserve gauge invariance while retaining the intended fermion content. Overlap fermions provide an exact form of lattice chirality, but leave a difficult problem: the allowed fermion subspace depends on the gauge field, and its integration measure needs a consistent phase throughout configuration space. Perturbative anomaly cancellation alone does not construct that phase globally.

Craig proposes a measure construction for a class of four-dimensional nonabelian theories containing the Standard Model. The central idea is to compare chiral projectors on successive auxiliary refinements of the same gauge configuration. Each comparison contributes to a measure current, which specifies the measure’s response to changing a gauge link. The refinement is an auxiliary construction; the physical fermion operator remains on the original lattice. Anomaly cancellation supplies the decay needed to sum the comparison currents while retaining exponential locality.

The global argument uses the same comparisons: phases accumulated between successive refinements telescope, leaving the original projector’s return phase and a residual fine-lattice contribution. The paper argues that this establishes the consistency conditions needed for Lüscher’s reconstruction of a smooth, gauge-invariant measure. The construction assumes admissible gauge fields, with sufficiently small plaquette deviations. Reflection positivity compatible with that restriction, remaining phase choices between disconnected components, practical implementation, and the interacting continuum theory remain open.

**Background.** The relevant geometry resembles Berry-phase transport, with gauge configurations taking the role of external parameters. Choosing a basis for the allowed chiral subspace fixes a local integration element, but changing that basis introduces a determinant phase. A measure current must compensate this ambiguity. Its infinitesimal curvature must agree with the projector geometry, and its integrated phase around every closed loop must reproduce the basis’s return phase. These are separate local and global requirements. Admissibility keeps the overlap operator smooth by preventing its defining Hermitian operator from crossing zero; it does not require every individual link to lie near the identity.

**Why it matters?** This addresses a structural obstacle to defining chiral gauge theories beyond perturbation theory. The contribution is a proposed construction of the regulated measure, whose detailed consistency deserves scrutiny independently of the subsequent continuum problem. For foundational QFT, the substantive connection is the passage from anomaly cancellation as a consistency test to an explicit local regulator construction. Establishing the remaining positivity and continuum properties would turn that advance into a more complete nonperturbative definition.

## arXiv-2026-10-08 — Item 2

### Correspondence between Asymptotic Quantum Many-Body Scars in Closed Systems and Diffusive Nambu-Goldstone Modes in Open Systems
**Masaya Kunimi, Taiki Haga, Masaya Nakagawa — arXiv:2610.09662**

Slow relaxation can arise because an isolated system supports exceptional nonthermal states, or because a conserved density must redistribute diffusively in an open system. These explanations usually involve different objects: energy eigenstates and their nearby states on one side, decay modes of a density matrix on the other. This paper identifies a common positive-operator structure that relates the two mechanisms under explicit conditions.

The authors construct asymptotic rainbow scar states in broad classes of spin, fermionic, and bosonic models. These are states orthogonal to the exact scar sector whose energy variance vanishes with system size, allowing increasingly long-lived nonthermal dynamics. The construction passes through a parent Hamiltonian whose zero-energy states are the rainbow scars. Within an enlarged scar subspace, this parent is a sum of local positive terms, each separately annihilating the scars. A long-wavelength variational excitation then gives an upper bound proportional to \(k^2\), where \(k\) is its wave number, while satisfying the required orthogonality and entanglement conditions.

On the open-system side, strong local charge dephasing suppresses coherences and leaves slower effective transitions between charge configurations. The effective Lindbladian contains the same local projectors as the parent Hamiltonian, with positive transition-dependent weights. When those weights remain uniformly bounded above and below, the spectra obey two-sided comparison bounds and their gaps have the same system-size scaling. Uniform weights make the operators proportional, so their eigenmodes coincide. An exact quadratic branch then maps to diffusion; the general variational upper bound by itself does not establish such a branch.

**Background.** Vectorization sends an operator \(|a\rangle\langle b|\) to a vector \(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\) in a doubled space. Consequently, the identity restricted to a charge sector becomes an equal-weight sum of paired configurations in the two copies: the rainbow state. This makes the stationary mixed state and the scar state manifestations of the same vector. The frustration-free parent penalizes local differences between configurations while leaving their appropriate superposition untouched, resembling a reversible stochastic generator. Strong-to-weak symmetry breaking supplies the mixed-state interpretation: nonlinear density-matrix correlators can retain long-range charge-transfer order even when ordinary correlators do not. The fixed-charge infinite-temperature states exhibit this order at finite density, and their slow charge modes acquire a Nambu–Goldstone interpretation.

**Why it matters?** The conceptual advance is an operator-level bridge between exceptional coherent dynamics and dissipative hydrodynamics. It lets spectral information obtained in one setting constrain relaxation in the other, while making clear which assumptions support only gap comparisons and which support identical modes. This offers a productive connection between solvable structures and non-equilibrium physics: parent-Hamiltonian methods become tools for studying open-system slow modes, rather than merely constructing special closed-system states.

## arXiv-2026-10-08 — Item 3

### A Superfluid at All Temperatures
**Sven Harder, Adar Sharon — arXiv:2610.10169**

Heating often destroys continuous symmetry breaking, but the competition is governed by free energy rather than disorder alone. A convincing counterexample in relativistic QFT must keep the potential stable, control thermal corrections, and avoid relying on infinitely many fields. Harder and Sharon construct local, unitary \(3+1\)-dimensional conformal theories with finitely many fields whose thermal states break a continuous global symmetry at every nonzero temperature.

Their starting point is a two-sector gauge–scalar theory whose planar limit has a circle of RG fixed points, with an arc supporting thermal order. Finite-rank corrections generally destroy a continuous family of fixed points, so the crucial question is whether any surviving isolated points remain on that ordered arc. The authors show that suitable integer choices of gauge ranks and flavor numbers achieve this. An infinite family reaches arbitrarily weak coupling, making both fixed-point existence and thermal ordering parametrically controllable. Each member has finitely many fields; improving control involves moving along the family to larger ranks.

The physical mechanism is entropic. A negative coupling between the sectors allows condensation in the smaller sector to lower the masses of many modes in the larger one. Their increased entropy can outweigh the energetic cost of ordering. Conformal invariance makes temperature the only thermal scale, so this competition does not reverse at some higher temperature. Weakly gauging the broken symmetry produces superconductivity over an exponentially large temperature range, with running couplings limiting that extension. Individual small members of the family have less secure perturbative control than the asymptotic construction.

**Background.** The fixed-point circle is a conformal manifold: different coupling values describe distinct scale-invariant theories. Here it arises from an accidental symmetry of the planar RG equations. Finite-rank corrections generate flow along the former circle, and its isolated zeros select the surviving CFTs. Thermal ordering is controlled by the scalar effective potential: a negative thermal mass destabilizes the symmetric configuration, while stable quartic terms determine the condensate. The paper arranges for this negative contribution to come from the numerous modes of the other sector and checks that higher-order effects become relatively small along the weak-coupling family.

**Why it matters?** This strengthens thermal symmetry nonrestoration from an idealized large-rank phenomenon into a controlled family of finite-field QFT examples. Its significance lies in clarifying what equilibrium relativistic field theory permits. The entropy mechanism is also a useful statistical-mechanical lesson: an ordered sector can be favored because it opens more thermally accessible states elsewhere. The superconducting extension is a further field-theory consequence; translating it into a material mechanism would require additional microscopic input.

### Gaussian Scrooge Ensemble from Deep Thermalization in Free-Fermions
**Angelo Russotto, Katja Klobas, Pasquale Calabrese, Bruno Bertini — arXiv:2610.10279**

A subsystem’s equilibrium density matrix does not determine the distribution of pure states obtained by measuring its environment. Different conditional ensembles can have the same average and different higher moments. For integrable dynamics, the question is especially delicate: conserved quantities constrain local equilibrium, while the measurement record may disclose additional information about them. This paper asks whether a universal conditional ensemble nevertheless emerges in a free-fermion chain.

The authors use local environmental measurements that preserve Gaussianity while breaking particle-number conservation. Their central argument is that the outcomes’ residual information about conserved charges becomes irrelevant to a fixed subsystem as the environment grows. The late-time ensemble should therefore depend on the subsystem’s generalized Gibbs state without retaining a separate macroscopic label for each measurement record.

They construct the proposed Gaussian Scrooge ensemble directly within the subsystem. Starting from an invariant distribution of Gaussian pure states, they filter each state with the square root of the subsystem’s generalized Gibbs density matrix, normalize it, and reweight its sampling probability by the filter’s squared norm. This guarantees the correct average density matrix and specifies higher statistics as well. Numerical comparisons of those statistics support the proposal at finite temperature. The emergence argument combines analysis with numerical evidence; it does not establish the same conclusion for interacting integrable dynamics or arbitrary measurement choices.

**Background.** The important restriction is the manifold of Gaussian pure states, which can be generated by quadratic fermionic transformations and represented through covariance matrices. Sampling uniformly on this manifold is much more constrained than sampling generic pure states. The Scrooge construction deforms that uniform distribution to accommodate a nonuniform equilibrium density matrix. Filtering alone would bias the average incorrectly; the accompanying probability reweighting restores it. Because the generalized Gibbs state is Gaussian, its square-root filter keeps the sampled states Gaussian. “Non-revelation” concerns what the measurement record tells us about subsystem charges: for example, measuring an environment’s exact particle number can constrain the subsystem number through a fixed total. The paper argues that the relevant constraints disappear locally in its large-environment limit despite finite-size charge information.

**Why it matters?** This gives a concrete subsystem-level description of thermalization beyond averaged observables in an integrable system. Its methodological value is that conditional-state statistics can be predicted without constructing and measuring a representative state of the entire environment. For non-equilibrium and measurement physics, the deeper issue is how conservation laws and measurement information jointly select an ensemble. Extending that principle beyond Gaussian dynamics would test whether a local generalized Gibbs state remains sufficient once interacting correlations become available.

### Particle-Number-Preserving Quantum Signal Processing for Thermal Properties: Canonical-Ensemble Estimation and Grand-Canonical Reconstruction
**Taehee Ko, Sungbin Lim, Sangkook Choi — arXiv:2610.09373**

Finite-temperature calculations require sampling many states, and the relevant fixed-particle-number sector can already be exponentially large. Quantum thermal algorithms face a related practical obstacle: their initialization, approximate dynamics, and thermal weighting must all respect the sector whose properties are being estimated. This paper assembles those components into an explicit framework and asks when its total cost improves on a classical Lanczos baseline.

The algorithm starts from a uniform mixed state within a chosen number sector, evolves using particle-number-preserving Trotter blocks, and applies generalized quantum signal processing to implement a thermal filter. Ancilla measurement probabilities determine the canonical partition function, while conditioned system measurements estimate thermal observables. Keeping the approximate evolution inside the sector allows the error analysis and spectral normalization to use that restricted space.

Sector-resolved estimates can then be reused across chemical potentials through
\[
Z(\beta,\mu)=\sum_n e^{\beta\mu n}Z_n(\beta),
\]
where \(Z_n\) is the canonical partition function for particle number \(n\), \(\beta\) is inverse temperature, and \(\mu\) is chemical potential. Observable numerators are reweighted similarly. Thus a chemical-potential scan becomes classical post-processing once the required sector data have been collected.

For Hamiltonians with extensive spectral norm and exponentially large relevant sectors, the authors identify system-size-independent temperature regimes with lower asymptotic cost than the specified Lanczos methods, up to polynomial factors. Two-dimensional Hubbard-model calculations illustrate this comparison. Circuit sampling remains temperature-dependent, so the result does not establish efficient access to arbitrary low temperatures or superiority over all classical thermal algorithms.

**Background.** A mixed Dicke state here means the uniform incoherent mixture of occupation strings with a fixed number of occupied modes. It provides an unbiased starting point for a trace over that sector. Generalized quantum signal processing interleaves ancilla rotations with controlled real-time evolution; interference synthesizes a polynomial approximation to a function of the evolution operator. Here that function approximates \(e^{-\beta H/2}\), up to normalization. Applying the filter on both sides of the initial mixture produces the desired Boltzmann weighting. “Block encoding” means this nonunitary filter appears as one ancilla-selected block of a larger unitary circuit. The probability of selecting that block is consequently part of the sampling cost. Number-preserving Trotter blocks ensure that finite-step approximation errors do not introduce contributions from unwanted sectors.

**Why it matters?** The useful advance is an integrated thermal-estimation method that treats symmetry preservation, approximation error, and sampling together. Reusable sector data are particularly attractive when exploring density-dependent many-body physics, because changing chemical potential need not trigger new quantum circuits. For quantum simulation and algorithms, the paper supplies a physically organized resource comparison. Demonstrating practical advantage would additionally require favorable costs at useful temperatures and comparison with the strongest classical methods for the model under study.

### Characterizing Fermionic Non-Gaussianity in the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev Model via Replica Twist Entropy
**Ning Sun, Pengfei Zhang — arXiv:2610.10123**

Interactions generate fermionic correlations that cannot be represented by Gaussian states, but diagnosing that departure requires more than measuring entanglement or a few two-point functions. The challenge is to probe higher-order structure with an observable that remains calculable in a strongly interacting model. Sun and Zhang introduce a two-copy diagnostic and use it to uncover dynamical structure in the Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev model.

Their replica twist entropy measures the response of two identical copies of a pure state to a continuous rotation mixing corresponding Majorana modes between copies. Replicated Gaussian states are invariant under this rotation; generic interacting states lose that invariance. Taking the negative logarithm of its expectation value produces an additive, Gaussian-unitary-invariant diagnostic that vanishes precisely for Gaussian states in the stated fixed-parity pure-state setting. At finite twist angle \(\alpha\), it accesses correlations of arbitrarily high order, whereas its small-angle limit reduces to a diagnostic determined by two-point data.

The authors develop large-\(N\) saddle-point equations for evolving thermofield-double states in SYK and solve them numerically. At the reflection-symmetric angle \(\alpha=\pi/4\), the solutions develop two symmetry-related branches with opposite replica-correlation order parameters. At late times, varying the angle switches the dominant branch and produces a cusp in the twist entropy. This is a transition in the replicated diagnostic within the large-\(N\) treatment. The construction and its established diagnostic properties concern pure states; a useful mixed-state extension remains open.

**Background.** The replica rotation acts like a beam splitter between two copies of every fermionic mode. Applying the same quadratic evolution to both copies commutes with this mixing, which explains why doubled Gaussian states retain the rotation symmetry. Expanding the rotation at a finite angle brings in products of arbitrarily many fermion operators, giving sensitivity beyond the covariance matrix. In the SYK calculation, the twist changes the free propagator entering the contour equations without destroying their large-\(N\) organization. Competing self-consistent solutions then act like thermodynamic saddle branches. The two diagnostic replicas are copies of the entire thermofield-double state; they are distinct from the physical and auxiliary halves used to purify a thermal density matrix.

**Why it matters?** The main contribution is a higher-correlation diagnostic with a workable route into interacting many-body calculations. The SYK example shows that resource-sensitive observables can reveal dynamical structure hidden from simpler correlation data. Together with Item 4, it sharpens a useful distinction for non-equilibrium quantum information: conditional-state universality within Gaussian dynamics and the production of correlations beyond Gaussianity require different probes. Whether the replica transition organizes comparably useful behavior in finite systems or other interacting models remains a substantive next question.

### Numerically exact simulation of open quantum networks with strong system-bath couplings using tensor network path integrals
**Quentin W. Richter, Moritz Cygorek — arXiv:2610.10259**

Strong environmental coupling makes many-body dynamics difficult in two directions at once: the system develops spatial correlations, and the bath preserves information about its earlier evolution. Tensor-network path integrals can represent both effects, but their intermediate tensors may become prohibitively large. Richter and Cygorek improve the contraction strategy so that environmental memory remains manageable in demanding examples.

Their single-site algorithm, CTEMPO, combines causal contraction order with early incorporation of the system propagators. Instead of first compressing a bath object capable of supporting many possible system evolutions, it uses the actual evolution while eliminating indices. The resulting matrix-product representation becomes shorter and more compressible. For a network, these local memory tensors form the teeth of a comb, attached to a backbone carrying correlations between system sites.

Spin-boson benchmarks show one to two orders of magnitude improvement in computation time over the compared methods in the tested regimes. The network algorithm handles a seven-site energy-transfer model with a sharply structured environment and collective emission from up to twenty quantum dots with individual phonon baths. These examples demonstrate capability beyond a weak-coupling memoryless description. The implementation treats local spin-boson-type environments; “numerically exact” means that discretization, tensor compression, and any memory truncation must be brought under convergence control. The benchmarks establish substantial improvements without implying uniformly small tensor dimensions for arbitrary networks.

**Background.** Integrating out a Gaussian harmonic bath gives an influence functional: a weight coupling the system’s forward and backward histories at different times. A process tensor stores this environmental response in a form reusable across system propagators and interventions. That flexibility can be expensive because it retains possibilities irrelevant to a particular evolution. CTEMPO incorporates system propagation during contraction, allowing those possibilities to be compressed earlier. In the comb representation, a tooth retains the local information needed about a bath’s history, while the backbone transmits correlations among sites. The speed gain comes from reducing these retained spaces before costly tensor operations, rather than approximating strong coupling by a weak-coupling master equation.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful numerical advance for determining when environmental memory qualitatively changes collective dynamics. It expands the set of open-system calculations that can serve as controlled benchmarks for simpler effective descriptions. The connection to non-equilibrium physics is concrete: structured baths can alter coherence and emission on timescales that local dephasing models erase. The method’s practical reach will depend on how spatial and temporal tensor dimensions grow in each application, but the demonstrated examples give a substantial starting point.

### Building codes with transversal CCZ using projective geometry and SAT solvers
**Bohan Lu, Kenneth R. Brown — arXiv:2610.09341**

Fault-tolerant computation needs non-Clifford operations with controlled fault propagation and reasonable physical-qubit cost. Encoding can make independent physical phase rotations implement an entangling logical gate, but arranging that action while maintaining error detection imposes restrictive arithmetic conditions. This paper combines a geometric construction with Boolean constraint solving to make those conditions tractable.

Lu and Brown construct CSS codes encoding three logical qubits in one block, with logical CCZ implemented by a pattern of single-qubit physical \(T\) and \(T^\dagger\) gates. Geometry first supplies an \(X\)-stabilizer space with suitable weight divisibility and guaranteed \(Z\)-distance at least three. A satisfiability solver then finds three compatible logical \(X\) operators. This separates a structured error-detection problem from the remaining search for the desired logical phases. The resulting constructions occur at thirteen block lengths between 48 and 496, with \(Z\)-distance three or four and overall distance at least three; the paper gives an explicit \([[48,3,3]]\) code.

The authors also prove that fewer than 39 physical qubits cannot suffice within a broader CSS class allowing transversal \(T\) followed by a diagonal Clifford correction. Such a correction may include entangling gates, making this lower-bound class broader than the new single-qubit \(T/T^\dagger\) implementations. A previously known 47-qubit code gives an upper bound in that broader class, leaving lengths 39 through 46 unresolved.

**Background.** In the binary projective construction, each qubit is assigned a distinct nonzero binary column of the \(X\)-check matrix. These columns are the syndromes of single-qubit \(Z\) errors. Nonzero columns exclude undetected weight-one errors; distinct columns exclude undetected weight-two pairs. Geometry therefore secures the stated distance condition before searching for logical operators.

The phase mechanism comes from arithmetic modulo eight. A physical \(T\) pattern assigns phases according to occupation weights, and the weight of a binary sum contains single-row, pair-overlap, and triple-overlap terms modulo eight. An 8-divisible stabilizer space means every binary sum of stabilizer rows has weight divisible by eight, helping remove dependence on stabilizer labels. The logical rows and physical signs must then leave the cubic phase \((-1)^{x_1x_2x_3}\), where \(x_i\) are the three logical bits, while canceling unwanted phases. The solver enforces the necessary overlap constraints. This explains how a product of physical single-qubit gates becomes a logical three-qubit interaction.

**Why it matters?** The contribution is a constructive method accompanied by a meaningful restricted resource bound. Fixing the stabilizer geometry before searching logical operators reduces the burden of simultaneously satisfying distance and gate constraints. For fault-tolerant quantum algorithms, this supplies concrete small-distance gate resources and a route to exploring their limits. Practical architectural value would additionally depend on syndrome extraction, connectivity, and complete logical-operation costs; the block length alone does not settle those questions.

### Vortex screening and the fate of the BKT transition with long-range couplings
**Guido Giachetti — arXiv:2610.10171**

Algebraically decaying interactions can favor true order in a two-dimensional XY model, competing with the algebraic correlations and vortex physics of the short-range BKT regime. Determining when that regime survives requires care: replacing the long-range cosine interaction by a quadratic approximation can change the vortex problem itself. Giachetti develops a real-space RG treatment that retains its full many-body vortex interaction.

For couplings decaying as \(r^{-2-\sigma}\), the calculation integrates out small vortex–antivortex pairs. At leading order in their fugacity, and within the dipole approximation, their screening preserves a representation in which the long-range vortex weight is averaged over Gaussian spin-wave fluctuations. This step does not expand in the long-range coupling. Fast spin waves are subsequently treated perturbatively near the quasi-long-range-ordered fixed line.

The resulting local stability condition has a transparent form:
\[
2-\sigma<\eta<\frac14,
\]
where \(\eta\) is the algebraic correlation exponent. The first inequality makes the long-range perturbation irrelevant, while the second prevents vortex proliferation. An open interval satisfying both exists only for \(\sigma>7/4\). Below that value, the retained flow has a finite-coupling critical point and predicts \(\nu^{-1}\sim\sqrt{7/4-\sigma}\) near its merger with the Gaussian line, with \(\nu\) the correlation-length exponent. The stability result concerns the neighborhood of that line; the finite-coupling exponent comes from truncated RG equations, and neither statement alone fixes every microscopic model’s global phase diagram.

**Background.** In the short-range model, separating a smooth field from vortex winding gives a Gaussian spin-wave sector and pairwise logarithmic vortex interactions. The nonquadratic long-range term couples these sectors and generates interactions among arbitrarily many vortices. Its Gaussian-average representation retains that dependence inside the averaged exponential instead of replacing it by a pair potential. Small vortex dipoles polarize in the field of more distant defects and screen their interaction. The paper shows that their leading contribution can be absorbed into the stiffness and fugacity while retaining this representation. This closure under vortex screening is the specialized step that makes the subsequent stability analysis possible.

**Why it matters?** The methodological advance is to preserve the relevant vortex interaction while obtaining tractable RG information. It gives a more carefully delimited basis for discussing the survival of BKT behavior with algebraic tails, and separates local universality arguments from claims about entire phase diagrams. This is valuable for field-theoretic treatments of defects and for quantum simulators with long-range couplings: an observed crossover or apparent algebraic regime must be interpreted with both perturbation relevance and access to its basin of attraction in mind.

### Root systems and Minimal RG flows to c=1 CFTs
**Changrim Ahn, Sara Kostadinovic, Francesco Ravanini — arXiv:2610.08938**

An integrable RG trajectory contains more information than its ultraviolet and infrared fixed points: scattering data can describe the crossover between them. Yet thermodynamic Bethe ansatz equations proposed from symmetry or endpoint information need an underlying scattering construction to establish their consistency. This paper provides that construction for a family of massless flows ending at central-charge-one theories.

The authors extend Dorey’s root-system method from massive scattering to amplitudes for massless right- and left-moving particles. The key is to retain the algebraic exponents that enforce fusion consistency while changing the elementary amplitude blocks appropriate to right–left scattering. Root geometry then ensures that the resulting amplitudes satisfy the bootstrap equations, which demand compatible descriptions of scattering involving fused particles.

For the minimal \(D_n\) coset theories, whose infrared descriptions have compact-boson radius \(\sqrt{n/2}\), the authors derive explicit amplitudes and their TBA equations. Relations among the root-system exponents allow the scattering kernels to be converted into the previously conjectured universal TBA form. This identifies the minimal flows \([D_n]_2\to[D_n]_1\) for \(n\geq4\), with the subscript labeling the coset family’s level. The result concerns these particular integrable scattering solutions; it does not classify general deformations of a compact boson.

**Background.** A Coxeter element is a fixed product of reflections associated with the simple roots of a Lie algebra. Repeated application groups roots into finite orbits, which label particle species in Dorey’s construction. Allowed fusion processes correspond to choosing roots from three such orbits whose sum vanishes. Their relative angles determine the rapidity shifts in the scattering consistency equations. Because the scattering amplitudes use exponents built from these roots, the zero-sum relation makes the shifted factors cancel, turning geometry into a bootstrap proof. For massless flows one must distinguish right–right, left–left, and right–left amplitudes; the latter couple the two chiralities and encode the crossover. Logarithmic derivatives of the amplitudes provide the interaction kernels in the TBA, linking this algebraic construction to thermodynamics and RG endpoints.

**Why it matters?** This strengthens a family of exact RG descriptions by deriving their thermodynamic equations from consistent scattering data. The broader methodological value is a reusable geometric route to massless amplitudes, reducing reliance on guessing coupled integral equations. For interests in QFT and solvable models, it connects Lie-algebraic structure to dynamics between fixed points, where exact results can test perturbative or numerical descriptions of crossover behavior. Extending the construction to other algebras would determine how general that route is.

### Efficient Estimation of Logical Sensitivities Through Fault-Counting
**Winston Fu, J. Wilson Staples, Jeff D. Thompson — arXiv:2610.10531**

A logical failure probability tells us how well an error-correcting circuit performs, but gives limited guidance about which physical noise mechanisms most need improvement. Estimating that response by finite differences requires multiple simulations and amplifies statistical noise when the parameter changes are small. This paper extracts all first derivatives from the fault records of a single Monte Carlo dataset.

The authors differentiate the sampling probability of each fault configuration while keeping the decoder’s decision rule fixed. Each simulated failure is weighted by how its probability changes with a physical noise parameter. The same shots therefore estimate every component of \(\nabla_{\mathbf p}p_L\), including sensitivities resolved by individual fault location. These components can later be grouped by noise mechanism, qubit, or circuit region.

Circuit-level surface-code benchmarks agree with finite differences and require one to two orders of magnitude fewer shots for comparable variance in the tested configurations. The authors use the derivatives to construct error budgets, resolve spatial sensitivities, estimate local effective-distance scaling, and drive Newton searches for threshold contours in multidimensional noise models. The estimator requires access to simulated underlying faults and their probabilities; hardware syndrome records alone do not supply that information. Its derivative describes the specified noise model and fixed decoder, rather than automatically including changes from retuning the decoder.

**Background.** Let \(f\) denote a sampled fault configuration, \(P_{\mathbf p}(f)\) its probability, and \(F(f)\) the indicator of logical failure. The score-function identity gives
\[
\frac{\partial p_L}{\partial p_i}
=\mathbb E\!\left[F(f)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\log P_{\mathbf p}(f)\right].
\]
It moves the derivative onto a known probability distribution, avoiding any derivative of the discrete decoding outcome. For \(M\) independent fault opportunities sharing probability \(p\), a shot containing \(m\) faults has score \(m/p-(M-m)/(1-p)\). Both occurring and absent faults contribute. Averaging this score over failing shots measures whether those configurations respond unusually strongly to the parameter. The paper applies this identity to the simulator’s physical fault distribution, making sensitivity estimation an additional use of information already present during sampling.

**Why it matters?** This is a practical methodological improvement that makes detailed logical response information much cheaper to obtain. It can turn a performance simulation into a diagnostic of where hardware or circuit changes would have the largest local effect. The connection to statistical response theory is especially useful for fault-tolerance studies with heterogeneous noise, where a single effective physical error rate hides consequential differences. Applying those gradients to engineering decisions still requires a noise model that captures the relevant device behavior.

### Geometric heat pumping on a quantum processor
**Gerónimo J. Caselli, Luis O. Manuel, Liliana Arrachea, Pablo S. Cornaglia — arXiv:2610.09062**

A cyclic drive can transfer heat between equal-temperature reservoirs even without a static thermal bias. In the slow-driving limit, the transferred heat can depend only on the oriented path through control space. Demonstrating this geometric response requires measuring small energy exchanges while separating pumping from the dissipation produced by finite-speed driving.

The authors implement a driven working qubit and two thermal reservoir ancillas on a superconducting quantum processor. The drive changes both the working qubit’s energy splitting and its relative coupling to the two reservoirs. Lowering the splitting favors heat absorption, while raising it favors release; arranging for these stages to couple preferentially to different reservoirs produces transport over a cycle. Repeated interactions with freshly prepared ancillas realize the bath dynamics, and energy measurements before and after each interaction provide a calorimetric record.

Reversing the control contour isolates the orientation-odd part of the exchanged heat. Its dependence on cycle period and contour agrees with exact collision-model simulations without adjustable parameters. As driving becomes slow, it approaches the geometric prediction of the corresponding Lindblad dynamics. At finite period, the orientation-odd signal can still contain nonadiabatic corrections, so contour reversal alone does not make the measured heat purely geometric. The experiment tests transport between emulated reservoirs, with preparation and driving supplied by external controls.

**Background.** A collision model replaces a reservoir by a sequence of independently prepared environmental units. Here each ancilla exchanges an excitation with the working qubit through a partial-swap gate, is measured, and is reset before reuse. This makes individual energy transfers accessible while preventing bath correlations from carrying over between collisions. In the continuous slow-driving description, the geometric heat has the form
\[
Q^{\mathrm{geo}}_\alpha=\oint\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\alpha(\boldsymbol B)\cdot d\boldsymbol B,
\]
where \(\boldsymbol B\) specifies the controlled qubit field and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\alpha\) is the response associated with reservoir \(\alpha\). The line integral changes sign under path reversal and is independent of traversal speed. The leading dissipative correction is even under reversal, explaining why comparing opposite orientations is an effective way to isolate pumping.

**Why it matters?** This makes a geometric principle of open-system thermodynamics experimentally testable through direct energy accounting on a small processor. Its value lies in connecting a microscopic repeated-interaction model, a master-equation limit, and measured transport within one controlled setting. For non-equilibrium physics and quantum simulation, it illustrates how processors can probe response geometry as well as Hamiltonian dynamics.
