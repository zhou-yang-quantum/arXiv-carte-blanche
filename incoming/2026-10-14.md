# arXiv-2026-10-14

This historical batch connects microscopic structure to collective behavior: entanglement hidden in small spectral weights, scrambling within hydrodynamics, and exact reductions of interacting transport. The quantum-computing papers examine complementary obstacles—constructing logical gates, controlling evolution errors, and extracting statistically useful observables. Long-range spin systems provide another thread, linking nonthermal dynamics and few-body universality to experimentally accessible simulators.

## arXiv-2026-10-14 — Item 1

### The R\'enyi area law conjecture is false below $\alpha=1$ in high dimensions
**Tomotaka Kuwahara — arXiv:2610.15199**

How much entanglement complexity can locality and a spectral gap exclude? The question matters both for classifying gapped phases and for understanding why ground states admit economical numerical representations. In higher dimensions, an area law for von Neumann entropy would still leave open how tightly the entire entanglement spectrum is constrained. Kuwahara shows that this distinction persists even within the gapped phase of a product state.

The construction embeds a highly entangled component into a ground state with exceptionally small amplitude. Local Hamiltonian rules generate parallel computational histories whose rare configurations contain many Bell pairs across the cut. A shared energetically favored starting configuration allows this structure to coexist with a uniform physical gap. The resulting Hamiltonians have bounded local dimension, interaction range, and interaction strength, and unique ground states connected to product states by uniformly gapped local paths.

For every fixed $0<\alpha<1$, these states can nevertheless have volume-law Rényi entanglement while satisfying a von Neumann area law. The geometric qualification is important: two dimensions require suitable rectangular families, whereas in three and higher dimensions one hypercubic family works for all fixed indices below one. Matching bounds, up to logarithmic factors, on the high-accuracy rank cost of approximate ground-state projectors expose the same obstruction quantitatively.

**Background.** A history state encodes successive configurations of a computation in a stationary quantum state; local Hamiltonian terms enforce the allowed transitions between configurations. Here the difficult step is preserving a gap while allowing rare histories to generate extensive entanglement. The entangled component is identifiable by a predetermined product projection across the bipartition. Such a projection cannot increase Schmidt rank, so the complexity it reveals places a lower bound on the original state's small-weight tail. This makes the tail a physical obstruction rather than an arbitrary decomposition of the state. Approximate ground-state projectors provide a complementary viewpoint: suppressing excited states requires an operator whose Schmidt-rank cost grows with the demanded accuracy.

**Why it matters?** This is a conceptual counterexample to a universal entanglement constraint, with unusually strong control over the Hamiltonian assumptions. It shows how Rényi probes can distinguish spectral structure within a single gapped phase. For simulation, the consequence concerns accuracy-dependent compression: a complicated tail need not prevent a useful approximation at modest accuracy, but it limits what locality and a gap alone can guarantee as precision increases.

## arXiv-2026-10-14 — Item 2

### Out-of-time-order hydrodynamics and scrambling
**Marta Bucca, Akash Jain, Márk Mezei, Alexey Milekhin — arXiv:2610.16888**

Hydrodynamics explains the slow relaxation of conserved quantities, while scrambling concerns the amplification of an initially small perturbation. Can the same effective degrees of freedom describe both? A difficulty is that exponential growth occurs on a thermal time scale, where truncating the usual hydrodynamic derivative expansion is inadequate. Out-of-time-order correlators also require a more elaborate time contour than ordinary response functions.

The authors construct an all-derivative-order hydrodynamic effective theory on a contour with four evolution branches. Its central additional structure is a spatially local $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ symmetry of the effective clock fields. They derive the theory microscopically for a low-temperature, maximally chaotic SYK lattice—a lattice of interacting fermionic quantum dots—and determine its transport coefficients in that setting.

An application to energy-current out-of-time-order correlators produces growth with Lyapunov exponent $\lambda=2\pi/\beta$, where $\beta$ is inverse temperature and $\hbar=k_B=1$. This saturates the chaos bound in the intermediate growth regime, before scrambling invalidates the small-correction expansion. Agreement with a shockwave description checks how the contour theory encodes the growing disturbance. The microscopic control belongs to the stated SYK limit; ordinary hydrodynamic behavior alone does not establish the extra symmetry in another system.

**Background.** Four contour branches keep track of the interleaved forward and backward evolutions in an out-of-time-order correlator. Each branch carries its own effective clock. The $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ transformations are fractional-linear redefinitions of a suitable thermal clock variable. A common transformation across the contour is redundant, but a relative transformation of selected branches can carry physical information. This distinction allows exponentially growing and decaying relative modes to survive in the four-branch theory even when they disappear from ordinary response functions. These modes are the scramblons: collective disturbances whose interaction represents the effect of an early perturbation on a later measurement.

**Why it matters?** The advance is a controlled connection between transport and scrambling at the level of an effective action, including observables built from conserved currents. For nonequilibrium field theory, it provides a concrete way to ask which information-spreading phenomena follow from collective variables and symmetry. Extending that explanatory power requires identifying the appropriate symmetry and microscopic matching beyond maximally chaotic models.

## arXiv-2026-10-14 — Item 3

### From Syndromes to Computation: A General Framework for Logical Operations in Quantum Error-Correcting Codes
**Hunter Nelson, Arshag Danageozian, ChunJun Cao, Sophia E. Economou, Edwin Barnes — arXiv:2610.17612**

A code's ability to protect information does not by itself provide convenient operations on that information. This is particularly consequential when one block encodes several logical qubits: an available physical operation may act on the wrong combination of them. The authors approach gate design by asking how the syndrome patterns of physical Pauli rotations can enforce a desired encoded action.

They derive an exact codespace-preservation condition for products of commuting Pauli rotations, valid at arbitrary specified rotation angles. Grouping generators by syndrome turns preservation into algebraic constraints that apply to CSS and non-CSS codes. In the equal-syndrome Clifford specialization, the problem becomes a signed factorization of a stabilizer into rotation generators. More elaborate syndrome relations construct logical rotations higher in the Clifford hierarchy.

Applications include an addressed $T$ gate on the $[[16,4,4]]$ bivariate-bicycle code and analytic addressed operations on recursive hypercube codes. Here “addressed” means that the encoded action selects designated logical qubits within a block. The equal-syndrome constructions also obey $w_{\max}\geq\lceil d/2\rceil$: the largest physical generator weight must reach at least half the code distance. This bound belongs to that construction class. The fault analysis treats multiqubit Pauli rotations as elementary operations and explicitly stops short of a complete circuit-level fault-tolerance proof.

**Background.** The useful feature of a common syndrome is that affected stabilizer checks see the same set of rotations. Commuting a check through the gate reverses the signs of the rotations that anticommute with it. Codespace preservation therefore becomes a condition on the corresponding doubled-angle operation. At the Clifford angle, that operation reduces to a signed Pauli product, explaining why stabilizer factorization solves the constraint. Meanwhile, two generators with identical syndrome have a zero-syndrome product, which can represent a logical Pauli. Their combined support explains the distance-dependent weight obstruction. For analytic addressability, the paper uses Clifford algebra bases as coordinates in which operator products, commutation, and syndrome relations become tractable operations on index sets.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a constructive language for designing gates jointly with a code's stabilizer structure. Its value is methodological: syndrome relations become search rules and analytic design constraints, including explicit limits on physical support. For quantum error correction, the next consequential comparison is the complete implementation cost—rotation synthesis, intermediate correction, and circuit faults—against alternative ways to realize the same logical operation.

### Breakdown of thermalization in quantum spin systems with long-range interactions
**Filippo Caleca, Fabio Mezzacapo, Tommaso Roscilde — arXiv:2610.15530**

Closed nonintegrable systems are usually expected to approach thermal values locally. Very long-range interactions challenge the reservoir picture behind that expectation because distant portions of the system remain energetically inseparable. The authors investigate whether this can produce nonthermal dynamics without integrability or disorder localization.

They consider ferromagnetic spin models with interactions decaying as $r^{-\alpha}$ for $\alpha<d$, together with a field coupled to another spin component. Their proposed mechanism starts from an unusual paramagnetic thermodynamics: interactions transverse to the field contribute vanishing energy per spin, although their operator spectral width remains extensive. The authors argue that energy eigenstates cannot generically reproduce this behavior. In the models examined, atypical eigenstates instead retain stronger transverse correlations and can support dynamics very different from the thermal paramagnet.

The explicit support combines equilibrium calculations, finite-size diagonalization, and dynamical calculations for a one-dimensional long-range XY model. Quenches retain magnetization opposite to the field while developing collective correlations and multipartite entanglement. These results support a distinctive nonthermal scenario in the stated negative-energy-density regime. The broad exclusion of thermalization across the Hamiltonian family is a thermodynamic argument whose generality should be distinguished from the specific finite-size and variational demonstrations.

**Background.** Kac normalization divides the interaction by a size-dependent sum of couplings so that total energy scales with system size. It leaves the system nonadditive: interactions between two macroscopic pieces remain comparable to their internal energies. The paper's sharper observation concerns how correlations enter that normalized energy. Persistent long-range transverse correlations can sustain an extensive interaction contribution, whereas decaying correlations make its energy density vanish. Eigenstates and a thermal mixture can therefore have markedly different correlation scaling. A collective-spin description supplemented by spin waves helps interpret the dynamics: the global spin motion can remain active while the nonuniform fluctuations fail to act as an effective thermal reservoir.

**Why it matters?** The work develops a potentially broad route to nonthermal behavior rooted in interaction range and thermodynamic scaling. Its strongest contribution is the connection between that proposed mechanism and explicit correlated dynamics. This makes it relevant to understanding equilibration in quantum simulators, where collective interactions are natural. Establishing its full reach requires stronger control of large-system, long-time behavior; exploiting the entanglement additionally requires robustness to experimental noise.

### Finite temperature Fredholm determinants for dynamical correlation functions
**Oleksandr Gamayun — arXiv:2610.18750**

Exact determinant formulas for dynamical correlations are useful only if their physical behavior can be extracted. At finite temperature, even the asymptotic problem is delicate: knowing a decay rate or power law does not determine the amplitude needed for quantitative comparison. Gamayun addresses this missing information for a class of dynamical Fredholm determinants with a quadratic stationary point.

The method replaces a difficult spectral sum by an explicitly summable auxiliary family of effective form factors—matrix elements chosen to reproduce the relevant asymptotic data. The remaining problem is to determine an effective momentum-dependent phase shift. Its outer solutions differ on the two sides of the stationary momentum, and their joining region affects the finite normalization.

The paper solves this local matching problem with parabolic-cylinder functions. A connection to a high-temperature Gaussian random-matrix ensemble then fixes the normalization, agreeing with an independent Riemann–Hilbert calculation. Separately, the leading exponential behavior recovers the Euler-scale generalized-hydrodynamic prediction for the hard-rod dynamical structure factor. The result is an asymptotic analysis of the specified determinant class; reproducing a physical correlator requires the appropriate matching to that representation.

**Background.** The effective form factors belong to a solvable auxiliary free-fermionic family. Their phase shift and weight encode the thermodynamic information needed from the original problem, allowing the spectral sum to be performed before the matching is completed. Along a ray with fixed $x/t$, the oscillatory phase becomes stationary where an excitation travels at that ray's velocity. The two outer phase-shift solutions must be smoothed through a momentum window of width proportional to $t^{-1/2}$. Parabolic-cylinder functions supply this quadratic-saddle profile. The random-matrix connection works because the local matching equation also describes an equilibrium eigenvalue-density problem whose known normalization determines the otherwise missing constant.

**Why it matters?** This is a methodological advance in extracting quantitative dynamics from exact representations. The finite amplitude contains information that a leading hydrodynamic prediction leaves undetermined. For integrable nonequilibrium physics, the useful bridge is therefore precise: microscopic matrix-element data and saddle matching can recover transport while also supplying corrections needed to test where the hydrodynamic description becomes accurate.

### Exact Microscopic One-Body Reduction in the Symmetric Exclusion Process
**Olivier Bénichou, Aurélien Grabsch — arXiv:2610.18626**

A current fluctuation in an interacting particle system has a spatial structure: an unusually large transfer of particles is accompanied by a rearranged density profile. In the symmetric exclusion process, equations for this profile generate an infinite correlation hierarchy. A closed equation was known after hydrodynamic scaling, leaving an important question: does coarse graining create the simplification, or reveal an exact microscopic structure?

Bénichou and Grabsch establish the latter for the infinite one-dimensional lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping and independent initial occupations at two potentially different densities. They study the density profile biased by the integrated current. After normalizing its spatial gradients, the hierarchy closes into a scalar linear equation involving the free lattice propagator and a fixed discrete transform. This determines the complete finite-time profile, including relaxation from a density step.

A further transformation identifies the equation with a persistence problem for one auxiliary random walker. The known hydrodynamic closure follows by diffusive scaling, and the microscopic solution also supplies finite-time corrections. The reduction concerns this current-biased correlation profile; a corresponding simplification for arbitrary observables remains an extension to pursue.

**Background.** Write $Q_t$ for the integrated current and $\eta_r(t)$ for the occupation at site $r$. The object being solved is

\[
w_r(t;\lambda)=\frac{\langle\eta_r(t)e^{\lambda Q_t}\rangle}{\langle e^{\lambda Q_t}\rangle}.
\]

The parameter $\lambda$ favors histories with particular currents, and its expansion generates connected current–density correlations. The discrete transform in the closed equation becomes an ordinary reflection after a Poisson transform, which packages lattice values using Poisson weights. In that representation, repeated action of the kernel describes a single walker. Its persistence condition is specific: starting at the coordinate set by physical time $t$, it must remain below that threshold at intermediate steps. These auxiliary steps are distinct from the exclusion process's physical time. The construction encodes the interacting observable without replacing the underlying particles by independent walkers.

**Why it matters?** This is a strong exact-solvability result because it explains a macroscopic closure through an identity valid before any scaling limit. It provides finite-time transport benchmarks and a concrete mechanism by which many-body correlations become a linear problem. The broader opportunity is to identify which observables preserve this transform-and-reflection structure, thereby extending exact nonequilibrium calculations beyond the present profile.

## arXiv-2026-10-14 — Item 7

### Signal-to-noise problems in fault-tolerant quantum computation
**Hyunwoo Oh — arXiv:2610.15210**

Accurate quantum evolution does not ensure that a desired observable can be estimated economically. A correlator may become extremely small while individual measurement outcomes continue to fluctuate by order one. This separates the cost of implementing a many-body calculation from the cost of extracting its answer, even on an ideal fault-tolerant device.

Oh analyzes this measurement bottleneck and develops quantum versions of Stein control variates: corrections with exactly zero expectation that can be subtracted from an observable to reduce fluctuations. Commutators provide one family, while a discrete construction handles corrections diagonal in the energy basis. Exactly solvable examples demonstrate variance reduction. Their role is to establish and illustrate an estimator-design strategy; they do not establish efficient estimation for general interacting systems.

The precision requirement is essential. For independent measurements of an estimator with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, achieving relative error $\epsilon$ requires roughly $N=\sigma^2/(\epsilon^2|\mu|^2)$ samples. An exponentially small mean can therefore make relative precision expensive even when fixed absolute precision remains manageable. Coherent estimation protocols can improve scaling, but the examples do not constitute a lower bound covering every quantum algorithm or observable.

**Background.** Stein constructions exploit a stationarity identity to generate zero-mean corrections without knowing the target expectation. In the quantum version, if the state $\rho$ commutes with a Hermitian operator $X$, then $f=i[X,A]$ has zero expectation for Hermitian $A$. Thus $O-cf$ and $O$ have the same mean, while their variances can differ. A stationary or thermal state permits $X=H$. The commutator controls off-diagonal fluctuations in the corresponding eigenbasis. For diagonal fluctuations, the paper instead uses differences weighted by neighboring thermal populations; their expectation cancels by telescoping. Formal freedom to represent a correction leaves a practical optimization problem: finding and measuring it must cost less than the repetitions it saves.

**Why it matters?** The useful contribution is a resource-accounting correction paired with a concrete mitigation framework. For field-theory and many-body simulation, the relevant computational task includes an observable and an error criterion alongside state preparation and evolution. Whether the proposed control variates improve that full task depends on accessible operator families, optimization costs, and measurement overhead, making these the substantive next benchmarks.

### Theory of Trotter Error with Variable-Weight Schedules
**Henrique Ennes — arXiv:2610.18202**

Product-formula errors are often controlled by improving each short simulation step. Yet a calculation usually seeks the final evolution operator, and the small coherent errors generated at different times can interfere. Ennes asks whether varying the decomposition weights from step to step can deliberately arrange their cancellation at the endpoint.

The framework treats the unitary evolution geometrically. Local defects are first computed within each step and then transported through the intervening evolution before being combined. Two Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansions separate the error internal to a step from the additional error generated by accumulating these transported defects. This distinction gives the schedule a target: reduce the final combined defect rather than optimizing each contribution independently.

Under exact infinitesimal synthesis, the proposed variable-weight schedule improves the usual fixed-weight $O(N^{-1})$ endpoint error to $O(N^{-2})$ for $N$ steps without changing the basic pulse skeleton. Imperfect synthesis introduces a covering parameter describing how closely the admissible coefficients realize the required local corrections. The numerical evidence uses random $SU(2)$ targets. The stated improvement is therefore conditional on available coefficient control, with its numerical demonstration confined to that small setting.

**Background.** Transporting an error means expressing it in a common interaction frame. A small unwanted rotation inserted early in a sequence is conjugated by later pulses, so its effective axis at the endpoint differs from its original axis. Errors that look similar locally can consequently add or cancel differently after transport. Changing the step weights adjusts those final contributions. Exact infinitesimal synthesis means that the available coefficients can realize the small corrections required by the schedule; a restricted coefficient set leaves residual defects whose size depends on its coverage of the desired directions.

**Why it matters?** This offers a useful design principle for coherent simulation: the endpoint error itself becomes an object to schedule. Its potential value is greatest when pulse weights are inexpensive to tune and the required correction directions are available. For quantum simulation, the consequential extension is to show that the improved error law survives many-body constraints with favorable total gate or control cost.

### Few-Magnon Universality beyond Contact Interactions in Long-Range Spin Chains
**Yiran Huo, Pengfei Zhang — arXiv:2610.16126**

Long-range spin chains offer a way to realize few-body physics with an unconventional kinetic energy. Previous theory predicted Efimov-like three-body bound states for magnons with a fractional dispersion, but assumed contact interactions between them. Experimental spin couplings generally produce interaction tails as well as long-range hopping. The physical question is whether those tails preserve the proposed universality.

Huo and Zhang study two and three magnons with dispersion $\epsilon_k=u|k|^z$ and an attractive potential $V(x)=V_0/(1+|x|^\beta)$. Here $u$ sets the kinetic scale, $z$ controls the dispersion, and $\beta$ controls the interaction tail. Near a two-body resonance, perturbative analysis and numerical scattering calculations support the contact theory's leading low-energy form for $\beta>1$ in the studied fractional-dispersion regime. Numerical three-body spectra likewise support its asymptotic geometric bound-state scaling in the Efimov window, approximately $0.52<z<0.88$.

The result concerns low-energy universality after tuning to resonance. It allows short-distance details to move the resonance and the overall binding-energy scale while preserving the asymptotic scaling structure. The three-body evidence is numerical agreement with that structure, rather than direct access to an infinite tower of states.

**Background.** Different spin couplings play distinct roles. Exchange in the transverse plane lets a spin flip hop, determining the magnon dispersion; longitudinal couplings become interactions between the spin flips. Long-range hopping can give $z<1$, changing the scaling balance of kinetic and interaction energies even in one spatial dimension. A two-body resonance occurs when a bound state reaches zero binding energy. In the paper's tail analysis, the momentum-independent part of the interaction shifts the coupling needed to reach that resonance. After retuning, the remaining momentum dependence is subleading at low energy for the stated regime. This explains why a spatially extended interaction can share the contact model's leading scattering behavior. Efimov scaling is the corresponding discrete scale invariance of the shallow three-body spectrum.

**Why it matters?** This is a robustness result that makes an unusual universality class more credible as a simulation target. It connects effective few-body theory to couplings closer to those available in trapped ions. The broader theoretical interest is the separation between universal scaling and microscopic realization when the kinetic exponent itself is tunable. Observing several sufficiently shallow states would require control of resonance tuning, finite size, and spectral resolution.

## arXiv-2026-10-14 — Item 10

### Experimental observation of Higgs oscillations and their power-law decay across pairing regimes in a cavity QED quantum simulator
**Chitose Maruko, Haoqing Zhang, Eliot A. Bohr, Leah Huzjak, Anjun Chu, Victor Gurarie, Chengyi Luo, Ana Maria Rey, James K. Thompson — arXiv:2610.14171**

The relaxation of a pairing amplitude can reveal the excitation spectrum of a paired state. Theory predicts that its collective amplitude oscillations change their algebraic decay across weak- and strong-pairing regimes, but resolving this intrinsic dynamics experimentally is difficult because inhomogeneity and additional relaxation processes obscure the envelope.

The authors implement an effective pairing model using cold, nondegenerate rubidium atoms coupled through an optical cavity. An Anderson pseudospin mapping encodes empty and occupied pair states into two-level degrees of freedom, while optical and microwave control with periodic dressing adjusts effective interactions and single-particle energies. Improved coupling homogeneity makes the transient amplitude oscillations accessible after a quench.

The experiment observes persistent pairing together with decaying Higgs oscillations. The reported weak-pairing envelope has power-law exponent $0.65(8)$; strong-pairing oscillations decay substantially faster, with exponent greater than one. Their frequency follows the minimum quasiparticle excitation scale. These are measurements of an emulated pairing Hamiltonian, and the fitted exponents characterize the accessible dynamical regime. They support the predicted change in decay across pairing regimes without making every measured exponent an exact asymptotic value.

**Background.** In the Anderson mapping, one pseudospin represents a pair of fermionic modes: its two states mean that the pair is occupied or empty. A transverse spin component represents coherent superposition of those possibilities. Pair transfer between modes becomes spin exchange, and the pairing order parameter is proportional to the collective transverse spin, $\Delta\propto\langle S^-\rangle$. This is why an atomic spin simulator can reproduce pairing dynamics. A Higgs oscillation is an oscillation of $|\Delta|$ about its persistent value. Different pseudospins precess at different quasiparticle frequencies, so their collective amplitude can decay by dephasing even in collisionless dynamics. Changing the structure near the minimum excitation energy changes how those contributions add at late times, linking the envelope to pairing character. Periodic, or Floquet, dressing engineers the effective spin Hamiltonian used to access these regimes.

**Why it matters?** The contribution is an experimentally resolved dynamical diagnostic of an effective pairing model, with controlled access to behavior that is hard to isolate in material superconductors or fermionic gases. For nonequilibrium quantum simulation, it demonstrates how a collective relaxation law can probe underlying spectral structure. The next tests of broader applicability concern which features survive beyond the reduced pairing description and over longer observation windows with residual inhomogeneity and dissipation.
