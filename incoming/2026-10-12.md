# arXiv-2026-10-12

The twelve papers in this historical batch connect open-system dynamics, quantum information, and continuum field theory through concrete mechanisms: exact stochastic reductions, geometric constraints on encoding, boundary descriptions of measurement, and nonperturbative constructions of spectra and scattering bounds. Several contributions sharpen the interpretation of familiar diagnostics, while others develop methods whose usefulness can be assessed against exact solutions or explicit resource costs.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 1

### Exact solutions of non-Markovian dissipative Ising models
**Mohammad Maghrebi — arXiv:2610.12546**

A bosonic mediator can generate useful spin interactions while also storing information about the spins’ earlier motion. Replacing it by an instantaneous interaction therefore risks losing precisely the dynamics that determine an experiment’s optimal operating time. Treating this memory together with local spin relaxation is difficult because the latter need not commute with the mediated interaction.

Maghrebi solves a class of these problems by replacing the lossy bosonic modes with stochastic fields and reducing the calculation to individual-spin evolution. Averaging suitable products of the single-spin solutions reconstructs spin observables and correlations, including the mediator’s memory. The construction accommodates dephasing, relaxation, pumping, and a specified class of processes that generate coherence. It excludes a transverse field; the longitudinal structure is essential to the solvable equations.

The spin-squeezing example illustrates the physical payoff: memory and local dissipation change both the attainable squeezing and when it occurs. The framework also recovers correlated dephasing and nonreciprocal spin dynamics in its Markovian limit.

**Background.** The stochastic factorization is an algebraic representation rather than a decomposition into physical product states. Its single-spin matrices need not be positive or normalized, so their averaged tensor product can represent entanglement. Without a transverse field, the transverse spin components and the population-and-trace components form separately tractable equations. Local spin flips can then enter through averages over their histories, with a retarded kernel carrying the bosonic response. This is how a calculation organized around individual spins retains interaction-generated correlations and environmental memory.

**Why it matters?** The contribution is a controlled benchmark for interacting dynamics with two distinct sources of dissipation. For non-equilibrium quantum simulation, its value is the ability to identify when eliminating a mediator changes a useful physical prediction, rather than merely changing a microscopic description. Extending the solvable structure to appreciable transverse driving remains a separate challenge.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 2

### An Instability of the Quantum Markov Length
**David C. Newsom, Michael Levin — arXiv:2610.12929**

A length used to distinguish mixed-state phases should have a clear account of its response to arbitrarily weak local noise. Newsom and Levin show that quantum Markov length can fail this test even at a pure product state, where the initial correlations present no apparent difficulty.

Their explicit fermionic construction applies two layers of nearest-neighbor Gaussian channels to the vacuum. However close the channels are to the identity, the resulting conditional mutual information has an algebraic tail, giving infinite Markov length. They also construct a qubit counterpart and establish an instability result for translation-invariant states with a rank-deficient reduced density operator.

The example is deliberately singular. Unequal coupling weights or a finite-temperature initial product state remove the spectral zero responsible for its algebraic behavior. The conclusion is a counterexample to unrestricted stability, with no claim that generic weak noise produces infinite Markov length.

**Background.** Each local channel couples an ancillary fermion to the symmetric combination of two neighboring modes and then discards the ancilla. Equal weights leave a staggered mode uncoupled: destructive interference makes it a dark mode that remains empty. This produces a zero in the occupation spectrum. Although the correlation matrix is simple, entropy applies the nonanalytic function containing \(-p\log p\) to its eigenvalues. Approaching \(p=0\) can therefore generate algebraic finite-interval entropy corrections. The conditional-information combination retains this effect. Lifting the zero regularizes the entropy calculation, explaining the sensitivity to fine-tuning.

**Why it matters?** This is a conceptual constraint on phase definitions based on Markov length. A diagnostic intended to organize noisy states must distinguish robust information structure from singular behavior at the boundary of the density-matrix spectrum. The result makes spectral regularity a substantive issue in connecting local noise, recoverability, and mixed-state phases.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 3

### Error correction and quantum information spreading on sparse random graphs
**Giovanni Cemin, Marin Bukov, Andrea Solfanelli — arXiv:2610.12597**

Sparse connections extending across many length scales might improve quantum encoding without requiring the connectivity of a dense graph. The difficulty is identifying which geometric property controls the benefit: the number of neighbors alone does not describe how information propagates or how logical operators find shortcuts.

The authors study periodically repeated random Clifford circuits on two tunable sparse graph families. They find that spectral dimension, extracted from the low-energy graph-Laplacian spectrum, organizes information spreading and a transition between localized area-law and ergodic volume-law entanglement. The transition has long-range percolation criticality.

For a CNOT-based encoder, the coding problem separates into a logical operator that cuts all winding paths and one that follows a winding cycle. More connectivity makes cuts expensive while shortening cycles. Their competition produces a finite-size optimum at intermediate spectral dimension; its location shifts with system size. These results concern the specified architectures and circuit ensembles, rather than a general threshold theorem for sparse hardware.

**Background.** Spectral dimension describes the abundance of long-wavelength graph modes and thus the effective geometry seen by diffusion. A graph drawn on a ring can consequently behave very differently from a one-dimensional lattice. In the circuit construction, some edges transmit operator spreading while others block it, making connectivity of the transmitting cluster central to the dynamics. A winding cycle and a cut intersect oddly, supplying the anticommuting logical pair. The code distance is limited by whichever geometric support is cheaper, so better spreading does not imply uniformly better protection.

**Why it matters?** The useful advance is a physical account of competing coding costs in intermediate geometries. It connects non-equilibrium information spreading to logical protection while explaining why connectivity must be optimized rather than simply increased. Translating this principle into fault-tolerant computation will require accounting for noisy encoding, syndrome extraction, and the cost of realizing long connections.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 4

### The Quasiparticle Ansatz for Relativistic Continuous Matrix Product States
**Silviu S. Pufu, Benjamin T. Søgaard, Spyros Terlemes — arXiv:2610.13824**

A good variational vacuum does not automatically provide an efficient description of its particles, particularly when a theory also contains solitons connecting different vacua. For continuum field theory, obtaining these excitations without introducing a spatial lattice would remove a substantial layer of extrapolation and regulator dependence.

The authors extend relativistic continuous matrix product states with a quasiparticle ansatz. They insert a local variational deformation into the vacuum and superpose its position with a plane-wave weight. Projecting the Hamiltonian into this excitation space gives an effective eigenvalue problem. Using different degenerate vacua on the two sides of the insertion similarly accesses the soliton sector.

Sine-Gordon soliton and breather masses agree well with integrability, providing a stringent benchmark before the method is applied to nonintegrable \(\phi^4\) theory. There it maps particle and soliton spectra across both symmetry phases and identifies a regime with two stable particles. The second particle is resolved below the continuum, but its mass is not fully converged in bond dimension.

**Background.** The relativistic continuum ansatz uses finite auxiliary matrices to organize superpositions of excitations above a free relativistic vacuum. Its creation operators retain the free theory’s short-distance structure; the finite auxiliary dimension limits the variational correlations rather than imposing a spatial mesh. The quasiparticle construction explores local directions away from the optimized vacuum, then restores definite momentum by integration over the insertion point. A soliton requires a different asymptotic state at each end, which is why changing the vacuum matrices across the insertion matters. Increasing bond dimension tests the remaining variational restriction. Working directly in the continuum does not make this restriction disappear.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful methodological extension from vacuum energetics to physically discriminating outputs: stable particles, bound states, and topological excitations. It gives exactly solvable models a productive role as calibration for nonintegrable QFT numerics. Its eventual reach depends on convergence and on extending beyond the low-lying excitation spaces demonstrated here.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 5

### Locality-Preparation Time Tradeoffs in Dissipative Quantum Systems
**Philipp Westhoff, Sara Murciano, Mattia Moroder — arXiv:2610.13784**

Dissipative state preparation transfers part of the control problem into the environment: the desired state becomes a steady state of engineered jump operators. But an easily specified reservoir can require spatially extended couplings. The practical question is whether those couplings can be localized without making relaxation arbitrarily slow.

For quadratic fermionic systems, the authors exploit the freedom to combine quasiparticle annihilation operators that share the same vacuum. This preserves the target state while changing both the spatial profiles of the jumps and their decay rates. The resulting construction makes the locality–relaxation relation explicit.

At criticality in the Kitaev chain, improving jump tails to \(r^{-(n+1)}\) requires an asymptotic timescale \(\tau\sim N^{2n}/\kappa\), where \(r\) is separation, \(N\) is chain length, and \(\kappa\) fixes the dissipation strength. Both gapped phases instead permit exponential localization with a finite thermodynamic relaxation scale; a special parameter curve admits finite-range jumps. Here \(\tau\) comes from the slowest physically allowed asymptotic decay, so it does not directly give the time to a prescribed global fidelity.

**Background.** The key freedom is more general than a unitary change of jump basis, which would leave the dissipator unchanged. In a translation-invariant system, momentum-dependent weights \(\beta_k\) multiply the quasiparticle coefficients. Their Fourier transforms determine the jumps’ spatial tails, while \(|\beta_k|^2\) sets mode damping. At criticality, suppressing a momentum-space singularity requires \(\beta_k\) to vanish there. This smooths the spatial profile but weakly damps nearby modes, whose momenta approach the zero as the chain grows. In the gapped cases, localization can be achieved without this suppression. Fermion-parity superselection also removes the slowest formal coherence modes from physical fermionic relaxation.

**Why it matters?** The advance is an exact resource relation within a controlled preparation framework. It separates a cost caused by criticality from one that might have been attributed to topology, and explains that cost through the reservoir’s momentum filter. This provides a concrete reference for designing quantum-simulation protocols and for testing whether interacting preparations exhibit analogous restrictions.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 6

### Replica Transition at Measurement-Altered Ising Criticality
**Kabir Khanna, Sara Murciano, Romain Vasseur — arXiv:2610.13802**

Measurements of a critical ground state generate an ensemble whose universal properties need not follow directly from those of the original state. Replica methods offer a natural route to its nonlinear averages, but that route becomes problematic if changing replica number changes the phase of the auxiliary theory.

For measurements of the critical Ising order parameter, the transition occurs precisely at the physical Born-average limit \(Q=1\). The authors identify its boundary-field-theory mechanism and analyze the physical point directly, rather than continuing the infrared answer from either neighboring phase. Their central ingredient is an operator that changes the replicated boundary orientation and becomes exactly marginal at \(Q=1\).

This yields a perturbative analytic calculation of the universal constant in the measurement record’s Shannon entropy, previously determined numerically. The same description gives moments of measurement-averaged connected spin correlations near the projective fixed point. The entropy result is a controlled expansion, rather than an exact determination of the full post-measurement ensemble.

**Background.** In the ground-state path integral, measurement acts along a single imaginary-time slice. Folding across that slice turns it into a boundary shared by the replicated theories. Summing over a common measurement record couples the replicas and favors aligned boundary spins. Even near a fixed boundary, its orientation can switch along space; the insertion creating such a switch is a boundary-condition-changing operator. Whether these switches die out or proliferate determines the boundary phase. Their relevance changes across \(Q=1\), while marginality at that point requires a separate treatment. The universal entropy constant records this effective boundary physics after the nonuniversal extensive contribution is removed.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a mechanism for a replica-continuation failure and a constructive way around it. The broader contribution to measurement-altered criticality is the link between classical outcome statistics and the same boundary operators controlling post-measurement correlations. Applying it elsewhere requires identifying the appropriate projective boundary condition and its changing operators, not simply borrowing the Ising continuation.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 7

### Dissipative Majorana Chain---Anomalies and Non-Invertible Symmetries
**Hung-Hsuan Teh, Tokiro Numasawa — arXiv:2610.12607**

Anomalous symmetry relations constrain closed quantum systems without requiring a solution of their dynamics. It is less obvious how these relations survive dissipation, because a density matrix admits symmetry actions that have no direct counterpart on a single state vector.

Teh and Numasawa organize this problem by representing the Lindbladian on a doubled ket–bra Hilbert space. Comparing translation and fermion-parity algebras in untwisted and parity-defect sectors retains the lattice signature of the closed Majorana chain’s modulo-eight anomaly. The doubled description also exposes projective relations involving strong and weak symmetries that are intrinsic to open dynamics, and matches the lattice algebras to a doubled Majorana CFT.

They then construct spin descriptions. For even chains with collective energy dephasing, combining sectors and imposing parity projections produces an Ising Lindbladian with non-invertible Kramers–Wannier symmetries. Odd chains instead lead to an Ising description containing a duality defect. These are explicit results for specified dynamics and sector choices.

**Background.** Vectorizing \(\rho\) turns left and right multiplication into actions on separate factors. A strong symmetry acts independently on the ket or bra factor; a weak symmetry acts through their diagonal combination, corresponding to conjugation of \(\rho\). A parity defect changes the boundary twist, allowing anomalous relations to be detected by comparing sectors. The bosonization here includes a sector-dependent parity projection, often called a GSO projection: it retains the fermionic states compatible with a spin description. Translation need not preserve that retained space. Projecting its compatible components gives a duality operation that can lose invertibility because intermediate information has been discarded. This explains how an ordinary translation in the enlarged fermionic construction can descend to a non-invertible Ising symmetry.

**Why it matters?** The conceptual advance is an explicit route from lattice anomaly algebra to non-invertible symmetry in Lindblad dynamics. It connects modern QFT symmetry methods to open many-body systems at the level of a concrete construction. Establishing further dynamical consequences, such as restrictions on relaxation or steady states, requires using these algebras in additional arguments.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 8

### Lattice Monte Carlo of $λφ^4$ on Euclidean de Sitter
**Angelo Caravano — arXiv:2610.12547**

Light scalar fields in de Sitter space accumulate long-wavelength fluctuations that undermine straightforward perturbation theory. Stochastic inflation resums this infrared physics, but a direct nonperturbative check should integrate the other field modes as well. Conventional spatial discretization introduces another obstacle: preserving the curved geometry’s symmetries at finite resolution.

Caravano uses a spectral regulator on Euclidean de Sitter space, the four-sphere \(S^4\), and samples the resulting interacting theory by Monte Carlo. Truncating complete spherical-harmonic multiplets preserves the sphere’s rotational symmetry. For massless quartic theory, the measured zero-mode distribution reproduces the weak-coupling stochastic equilibrium, with deviations following the one-loop renormalization of the quartic interaction.

At strong bare coupling, the renormalized quartic coupling saturates near the one-loop flow’s ceiling. This supports a connection to the screening behind flat-space triviality. The evidence concerns equilibrium observables at finite spectral cutoffs; it does not independently settle Lorentzian real-time evolution or establish the continuum strong-coupling endpoint.

**Background.** A spectral lattice retains field modes rather than field values on a geometric grid. Complete harmonic multiplets transform into one another under rotations, so retaining all members preserves symmetry while cutting off angular resolution. The zero mode is the field averaged over the sphere. Its quartic potential stabilizes an otherwise problematic massless fluctuation, giving a non-Gaussian distribution. Sampling the full retained theory lets the nonzero modes modify this distribution, rather than treating the zero mode in isolation. The comparison with stochastic equilibrium therefore tests both the infrared treatment and the corrections generated by the remaining modes.

**Why it matters?** The principal contribution is a symmetry-preserving computational method with a substantive nonperturbative benchmark. For QFT, it offers a way to separate genuine infrared effects from regulator artifacts and controlled loop corrections. More extensive cutoff studies and more efficient sampling are needed to turn this demonstration into a general continuum tool for de Sitter physics.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 9

### Fast computational primitives for La-cross qLDPC codes
**Andrea Di Fini, Tom Hartweg, Laura Pecorari, Guido Pupillo, Samuel Crew — arXiv:2610.12685**

Encoding several logical qubits compactly is useful only if they remain accessible for computation. Logical gates, measurements, and resource-state injection can otherwise consume the savings achieved by the memory. This paper asks how much computational structure can be extracted from the internal geometry and symmetries of La-cross codes.

For a specified subclass, the authors show that symmetries of the classical seed code, combined with folded transversal operations, generate the full logical Clifford group up to Pauli corrections. They also prove that factorized logical Paulis have \(D\) disjoint physical representatives, where \(D\) is code distance, enabling parallel measurement constructions. An addressable surface-code interface supplies externally prepared resource states to chosen logical qubits.

Together these ingredients provide a Clifford-plus-\(T\) instruction set. The scope matters: the strongest algebraic results require the specified seed family, and some lifted seed symmetries require check-sector CNOT circuits. Fast measurement also trades time for ancillas; the general two-round resource estimate uses heuristic fault counting.

**Background.** A seed automorphism permutes classical code coordinates while preserving valid codewords. Lifting it to the hypergraph product changes the encoded basis while preserving the quantum code; its physical action can include more than a permutation. The square product also pairs qubits under transposition. A Hadamard fold combines physical Hadamards with exchange of mirror pairs. A phase fold applies phase gates on the diagonals and controlled-\(Z\) gates between mirror partners. These operations supply logical transformations missing from the seed symmetries alone.

The factorized, or rank-one, Paulis have logical matrix labels that are outer products of row and column vectors. Their disjoint representatives implement the same logical observable on separate supports. Gauging a representative introduces ancillas and local checks whose product equals that observable; parallel copies supply redundant outcomes without sharing data qubits. This turns spatial redundancy into a possible reduction in repeated measurement time.

**Why it matters?** This is a constructive step from a compact memory toward an addressable computational architecture. Its relevance to error correction lies in how code symmetry, logical support, and an external resource interface work together, rather than in a single favorable code parameter. Hardware-level benefit will depend on compiling the symmetry circuits, supplying ancillas, and validating the proposed measurement speedup under a full noise model.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 10

### An entangling Hubbard quasiparticle collider on a digital quantum processor
**Chan Bin Bark, Hanbyul Kim, Yoonil Koo, SeungBeom Hong, Seik Pak, Kwang Jun Ahn, Sangmo Cheon, Youngseok Kim, Moon Jip Park — arXiv:2610.13946**

Quasiparticle scattering probes interactions more directly than a static density profile, but preparing distinguishable excitations and following their collision is experimentally demanding. A programmable quantum processor offers control over their initial positions, motion, and interaction strength, making an exact scattering prediction accessible through a dynamical experiment.

The authors collide a doublon and a holon in a half-filled, 21-site Hubbard chain encoded on 42 qubits of the ibm_boston processor. The measured transmission agrees with the Bethe-ansatz scattering matrix without fitted parameters. A two-doublon control lacks the same interaction-dependent scattering signature, helping distinguish the roles of the two quasiparticle flavors.

The collision also produces particle-number fluctuations consistent with coherent transmitted and reflected branches. The authors interpret these as evidence of collision-generated entanglement. Their supplementary analysis explicitly treats the experimental signal as qualitative: noise and mixedness prevent the ideal pure-state argument from certifying an entanglement lower bound.

**Background.** A doublon and holon carry excess double occupancy and a vacancy relative to a singly occupied background. In the ideal scattering picture, transmission and reflection leave different charge configurations on the two sides of the collision. A coherent superposition therefore correlates the left and right halves. With fixed total particle number and a pure global state, the distribution of left-half particle number contributes to entanglement entropy. The experiment measures its variance, which tracks branch weights in the simplified scattering picture. A classical mixture can also fluctuate, however, and background dynamics contribute additional variance. These facts explain why the fluctuation signal is an informative probe without becoming a general entanglement witness.

**Why it matters?** The strongest demonstrated contribution is a controlled hardware measurement of an interacting quasiparticle scattering probability against exact theory. It links integrability to quantum simulation through a physically interpretable observable. Establishing the scattering amplitudes’ coherence more directly would strengthen the proposed use of such colliders to investigate entanglement production.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 11

### Critical Ising model as the $M(4,3)$ minimal model of conformal field theory
**Dmitry Chelkak, Konstantin Izyurov, Kalle Kytölä — arXiv:2610.13705**

The identification of critical Ising physics with the \(M(4,3)\) minimal model is foundational, but matching primary fields and a collection of exponents leaves a harder question: how does an arbitrary local lattice observable enter the full continuum state space, including descendants and exact relations between fields?

The authors construct a linear map from local spin polynomials to the minimal-model state space. Its kernel consists precisely of lattice null fields, which vanish in correlations with sufficiently distant insertions. For every non-null observable, the lowest scaling-dimension component of its image determines the power of lattice spacing needed for a nontrivial correlation limit. These limits are expressed through primary correlators and continuum Virasoro action.

Thus the constant, spin, and energy fields anchor a correspondence that extends to general local spin observables. The construction applies to critically weighted isoradial graphs, broadening its geometric scope beyond the square lattice while retaining specific geometric and coupling assumptions.

**Background.** The new bridge is an exact lattice action of fermionic current modes. Discrete contour integrals around a local insertion act on lattice fields in a way analogous to continuum fermion modes. Discrete complex analysis controls these operations and their continuum limits; a fermionic Fock-space representation then organizes the resulting states. Disorder insertions enlarge the intermediate field space so that the fermionic construction closes. Quotienting by null fields removes lattice expressions that cannot be distinguished by distant probes. Isoradial graphs have faces inscribed in circles of a common radius, giving a geometry adapted to the required discrete analytic structure. A microscopic observable can contain several scaling components, so its leading continuum dimension need not be apparent from its bare spin polynomial.

**Why it matters?** This turns a central physical identification into a systematic operator correspondence. Its significance for exact models and CFT is the control of general local fields and descendants, beyond checking a few universal quantities. It also clarifies which microscopic distinctions survive at long distances and which disappear as exact local relations.

## arXiv-2026-10-12 — Item 12

### The Real EFThedron: A Moment Geometry for the Nonperturbative S-Matrix
**Laurentiu Rodina, Anna Tokareva, Yongjun Xu — arXiv:2610.12615**

Positivity bounds give low-energy couplings a useful geometric description, but positivity of an absorptive part captures only part of scattering consistency. Full unitarity constrains real and imaginary amplitude components together. The challenge is to retain the tractability of moment methods while imposing this nonlinear information.

The authors extend the EFThedron construction by multiplying amplitudes by fractional-power kernels. Their branch-cut phases bring the real part into dispersive moment relations alongside the imaginary part. Unitarity then becomes a bounded complex moment problem, while analyticity requires different kernels to reconstruct the same low-energy coefficients. These consistency requirements yield linear constraints analogous to crossing null constraints.

In two dimensions, the moment problem is solved exactly and its extremal measures yield Castillejo–Dalitz–Dyson amplitudes. Four-dimensional applications give closed-form and semi-analytic bounds, including improvements on published dual bounds for massive scalar scattering. The four-dimensional regions obtained from finite constraints remain outer bounds: their boundaries need not be realized by physical amplitudes.

**Background.** In an ordinary positive moment problem, numbers such as \(m_n=\int x^n\,d\mu(x)\) must arise from a common positive measure. Positivity of matrices built from these moments constrains which sequences are possible. Dispersion relations put EFT coefficients into this form using scattering data as the measure. The extension retains the complex partial wave, whose allowed values lie in a unitarity disk, rather than using only positivity of its imaginary part. Fractional kernels rotate which combination of real and imaginary data enters each integral. Agreement between these reconstructions enforces analyticity. The two-dimensional CDD solutions are elastic amplitudes whose pole-and-zero structure supplies extremal examples; finite four-dimensional moment constraints alone do not establish comparable attainability.

**Why it matters?** The conceptual advance is a common geometric language for positivity, nonlinear unitarity, and dispersive consistency. It offers the S-matrix bootstrap more analytic structure than a collection of numerical exclusions alone. Its broader impact on mapping consistent QFTs depends on how efficiently additional constraints tighten the bounds and whether candidate boundaries can be matched by actual amplitudes.
