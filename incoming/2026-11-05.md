### Simulating a chiral gauge theory
**Laurens Lootens, Hersh Singh — arXiv:2611.06880**

Chiral gauge theories remain difficult to simulate because the lattice must preserve a delicate combination of chirality, gauge invariance, and anomaly cancellation. Existing constructions can require either troublesome fermion measures or infinite local Hilbert spaces. This paper develops a finite-dimensional Hamiltonian construction for abelian chiral gauge theories in one spatial dimension and demonstrates its use with tensor networks.

The authors start from the bosonized theory and replace continuous rotors with clock-and-shift qudits. The essential choice is to preserve an exact $\mathbb{Z}_N$ subgroup and its anomaly modulo the group order. When the anomaly cancels, the Gauss constraints can be imposed consistently and then solved, leaving an unconstrained qudit chain suitable for numerical work.

Their principal example is the 34-50 model. At $N=6$, infinite matrix product state calculations recover a central charge close to one and momentum and winding scaling dimensions consistent with a compact boson near the free-fermion radius. The small displacement from that radius corresponds to a weak Thirring interaction. This is evidence that a modest finite local Hilbert space captures the expected infrared theory; the exact microscopic gauge symmetry remains discrete.

**Background.** The name 34-50 specifies left-moving charges three and four and right-moving charges five and zero. Their gauge anomaly cancels because $3^2+4^2=5^2+0^2$. After bosonization, these charges become combinations of momentum and winding charges. Clock operators measure a discrete rotor coordinate, while shift operators advance it cyclically. Their noncommuting algebra retains the relevant anomaly information. Cancellation makes the local gauge generators mutually compatible, enabling the exact constraint reduction.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a concrete computational route from an anomaly-consistent lattice definition to nonperturbative infrared data. It is a substantial methodological advance at the intersection of QFT and quantum simulation. The demonstrated setting is one-dimensional and abelian; extending the construction to higher dimensions or nonabelian chiral theories remains a separate challenge.

### Quasi-Normal Modes and thermalization in integrable Quantum Field Theories from the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz
**Zoltan Bajnok, Romuald A. Janik — arXiv:2611.06473**

Knowing an integrable theory's scattering matrix does not make its real-time thermal correlators easy to calculate. Exponential damping emerges from infinitely many terms in a thermal form-factor expansion, including terms that grow with time at each perturbative order. The authors propose a way to obtain damping rates and oscillation frequencies directly from modified thermodynamic Bethe ansatz equations.

They package these quantities into a complex frequency, $\omega=\Omega-i\Gamma$, whose real part controls oscillations and whose negative imaginary part controls decay. Their starting observation relates thermal response poles to an analytically continued finite-volume spectral problem. The proposed continuation requires an unusual cut structure in rapidity space, rather than an ordinary excited-state continuation. Matching to form-factor calculations fixes additional source terms.

The construction reproduces the known thermal damping of the thermally perturbed Ising theory. For the magnetic E8 deformation, predicted frequencies, supplemented by two-particle contributions and fitted amplitudes, describe tensor-network response data well. The proposal remains conjectural: the basic equations do not yet generate the complete relaxation spectrum, and the suggested extension to generalized hydrodynamic backgrounds needs further validation.

**Background.** The thermodynamic Bethe ansatz converts scattering data into nonlinear integral equations for dressed particle energies and thermodynamics. Here it is repurposed as a complex spectral problem. A quasi-normal mode is a damped response frequency, familiar from black-hole perturbations but meaningful without a gravitational dual. Secular terms are contributions whose time growth invalidates a fixed-order expansion; resumming them can generate exponential decay. The proposed equations aim to perform that resummation implicitly, leaving operator-dependent amplitudes outside the frequency calculation.

**Why it matters?** If established more generally, this would turn a difficult dynamical resummation into a tractable integrability calculation. Its appeal lies in connecting exact scattering, thermal response, and holographic intuition. The present checks make it a serious proposal, while an all-order derivation and tests in further interacting theories are needed to establish its reach.

### Statistical mechanics and hierarchy of learnability in quantum codes: From generalized symmetries to non-Abelian topological orders
**Hiromi Ebisu, Masazumi Honda — arXiv:2611.06154**

Local weak measurements reveal noisy fragments of information about a code state. Which nonlocal observables can be inferred from those fragments, and can their learnability vary independently? This paper derives structural restrictions on the possible answers, using statistical mechanics to connect inference to operator multiplication and anyon structure.

For CSS stabilizer codes, stabilizer relations determine symmetries of an associated classical spin model. Matching Gaussian measurement strengths to the deformation strengths of the initial wavefunction produces a higher Nishimori manifold: a special parameter set with an enlarged permutation symmetry among replicas. On this manifold, exact identities turn record-averaged squared posterior expectations into correlations of a clean model.

When that clean model has nonnegative ferromagnetic couplings, correlation inequalities imply a hierarchy: if two observables remain learnable at long distances, their product must remain learnable too. The converse need not hold. The authors develop examples in the rank-2 toric code and X-cube model, then extend the framework to non-Abelian quantum doubles, where representation kernels and fusion impose further restrictions under additional positivity conditions.

**Background.** The posterior expectation is the optimal conditional estimate of an observable after seeing a measurement record. Squaring it before averaging prevents opposite inferred signs from canceling, so it measures recoverable information rather than ordinary average order. The higher Nishimori symmetry identifies this inference diagnostic with a correlation before conditioning on records. That identification is the bridge allowing clean ferromagnetic inequalities to constrain a noisy learning problem. In the nonabelian extension, electric charges carry matrix representations, so character correlations and representation structure replace a single binary order parameter.

**Why it matters?** The contribution is a set of exact constraints on learning phase diagrams, beyond calculating another threshold. It connects measurement, generalized symmetry, and the organization of fractionalized information. The strength is also its boundary: the hierarchy relies on matched parameters and positivity, and should not be assumed for arbitrary noisy measurements or arbitrary nonabelian channels.

### Noise-Induced Topology in Chiral Quantum Walks
**Alexandre Chaduteau, Derek K. K. Lee, Frank Schindler, Abhinav Prem — arXiv:2611.05471**

Dephasing usually weakens interference-based directional transport. Can it instead create a robust organizing principle for relaxation? The authors show that ordinary fluctuations of on-site energies can generate topological structure in a chiral quantum walk, even when the corresponding evolution conditioned on no jumps has no such topology.

The mechanism appears in the closed equation for two-point correlations. Although the full Lindbladian is interacting, this equation can be viewed as a two-body problem for the two correlation indices. Noise couples those indices attractively, producing bound states. Their complex relaxation eigenvalues form a band with point-gap topology, and its winding determines the direction of the late-time asymmetry.

In a dephased flux chain, the density center remains fixed while its third cumulant grows linearly in time. With boundaries, the relaxation eigenmodes accumulate near one edge, producing a non-Hermitian skin effect without ordinary density drift. Multiband models allow dephasing strength to reverse the winding and asymmetry. A ladder construction also supports directed motion of coherences.

**Background.** A point gap means that a band of complex eigenvalues avoids a reference point and can wind around it. This differs from a real-energy band gap. Here the band belongs to the correlation generator and describes decay as well as oscillation. The noise-induced bound state binds the two indices of a correlation matrix; it is not a bound pair of physical particles. Its long-wavelength dispersion controls spatial cumulants, explaining how topology can appear in asymmetric broadening while the density's center stays stationary.

**Why it matters?** This establishes an analytically accessible mechanism through which decoherence creates dynamical topology. It is particularly interesting for non-equilibrium physics because the topology belongs to relaxation modes and has observable transport consequences. Robustness applies within the stated class of dynamics and perturbations; the work does not establish protection against every change to the noise model.

### Stability of energy gap of macroscopic quantum matter
**Kangle Li, Liujun Zou — arXiv:2611.06528**

The notion of a gapped phase assumes that sufficiently weak local perturbations cannot immediately close the gap. Finite-dimensional perturbation theory does not establish this for macroscopic matter, because a weak perturbation at every site has an extensive total strength. This paper examines how much locality and translation invariance actually guarantee.

The authors construct local Hamiltonians with unique gapped ground states whose gaps close under arbitrarily weak perturbations in the thermodynamic limit. The instability exploits configurations containing an extensive number of particle-like defects but only subextensive excitation energy: a weak energy gain per defect can overturn that balance.

They then prove a sharper one-dimensional result. Within their specified local Hamiltonian families, translation-invariant spin and fermionic systems with unique uniformly gapped ground states have stable gaps. One-dimensional unstable examples must break translation invariance and are fine-tuned: suitable arbitrarily weak perturbations can stabilize them. Above one dimension, they construct translation-invariant unstable examples as well.

**Background.** Stability requires a perturbation tolerance that survives increasing system size; a separate tolerance for each finite system is insufficient. The one-dimensional proof uses a doubled ground state and its complex conjugate, which can be disentangled by finite-duration quasi-local evolution. In that product-state description, defects can be counted locally. Controlling energy in terms of defect number supplies the missing link between local perturbation strength and a uniform spectral bound. The constructions distinguish a fragile Hamiltonian from a robust neighborhood of Hamiltonians.

**Why it matters?** This addresses a foundational assumption behind phase classification and adiabatic continuation. The combination of counterexamples and a broad one-dimensional stability result is more informative than either alone. Its implications for QFT and quantum information concern when locality supports a stable phase; the results should be read with their uniqueness and uniform-gap assumptions, rather than extrapolated directly to arbitrary degenerate topological ground spaces.

### Zero noise extrapolation of transport coefficients
**Hansveer Singh, Pieter Claeys, Sarang Gopalakrishnan, Anushya Chandran — arXiv:2611.05481**

Transport is an unusually delicate target for zero-noise extrapolation. The conservation law defining diffusion can be broken by arbitrarily weak noise, and long-time hydrodynamic fluctuations can make the approach to zero noise nonanalytic. Consequently, an ordinary polynomial fit can give the wrong diffusion constant even with accurate noisy data.

The authors first define an effective transport coefficient from the momentum-dependent part of a decay rate, separating it from the uniform decay of the approximately conserved charge. They then show how hydrodynamic long-time tails change the extrapolation variable. In their one-dimensional examples, the leading behavior can take the form $D_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma)=D_0+a\sqrt{\gamma}+\cdots$, with $\gamma$ the noise strength. The intercept gives the intended noiseless coefficient when the relevant stationary-state matching is satisfied.

Analytical predictions are benchmarked for dissipative spin transport and a dipole-conserving model with subdiffusion. A separate issue is equally consequential: noise can select a stationary ensemble different from the desired noiseless one. The paper discusses preparation and time-window choices needed to extract the appropriate linear response.

**Background.** A transport coefficient integrates current correlations over time. Nonlinear hydrodynamics produces an algebraically decaying tail in those correlations. Noise cuts that tail off at a time of order $1/\gamma$, so the omitted tail becomes a fractional power of noise strength. This is an infrared effect, not simply an inaccurate microscopic noise calibration. Separating the uniform charge-decay rate isolates spatial spreading, but it does not by itself repair a mismatch between stationary ensembles.

**Why it matters?** This provides a physically motivated extrapolation strategy and explains why familiar fitting procedures can fail. It connects a practical quantum-simulation problem to universal non-equilibrium fluctuations. The useful lesson extends beyond the specific examples: the correct extrapolation must respect both the hydrodynamic singularity and the state in which the transport coefficient is defined.

### Universally Certifying Partially Self-Correcting Quantum Memories in 3D Translation-Invariant Stabilizer Codes
**Chong-Yuan Xu, Yong Xu — arXiv:2611.06469**

Passive quantum memories need local interactions that obstruct thermally generated logical errors. For three-dimensional stabilizer models, recognizing that obstruction usually requires a separate analysis of each code. This paper develops a computable algebraic criterion that turns the local stabilizer specification into a certificate for a logarithmic logical energy barrier.

The authors work with trivariate bicycle codes, a polynomial description encompassing the cubic code and further translation-invariant families. Algebraic curves associated with the generating polynomials carry information about possible excitation transport. Orders of zeros and poles of their coordinate functions form a valuation matrix. Full rank on the relevant components, together with the other stated algebraic conditions, enforces the required restriction on string-like transport.

Combining this barrier with an efficient recovery procedure gives partial self-correction under local Pauli Davies dynamics. The framework certifies additional families, including models with stabilizer weight six. Numerical lifetime studies support the usefulness of finite examples, although some reported lifetime data are lower bounds from unfinished trajectories.

**Background.** A monomial $x^a y^b z^c$ encodes a lattice translation through its three exponents. A valuation records how a coordinate function vanishes or diverges at a boundary point of an algebraic curve. The matrix collects these directional measurements. Full rank means that bounded endpoint data constrain displacement in every spatial direction, obstructing arbitrarily long strings with small charged endpoints. This connects algebra to the no-strings condition and then to an energy barrier. Partial self-correction means that an appropriate temperature-dependent finite size yields super-Arrhenius lifetime growth; it does not promise unlimited improvement with size at fixed temperature.

**Why it matters?** This offers a systematic route to certifying and designing passive memories, with a clear bridge from local code structure to thermal dynamics. Its scientific value is the computable transport criterion. Practical advantages still depend on realizable interactions, bath couplings, and recovery, while the lifetime guarantee is explicitly tied to the specified thermal dynamics.

### Chiral edge modes from lattice Chern-Simons theory
**Gordon W. Semenoff — arXiv:2611.05663**

A lattice description of a topological field theory should reproduce its boundary structure as well as its bulk states. For Chern-Simons theory, the challenge is to retain chiral boundary degrees of freedom without introducing unwanted bulk dynamics. Semenoff analyzes a spatial lattice formulation and derives its edge structure directly by reducing the bulk constraints.

The theory uses a square spatial lattice and continuous time. On a cylinder, solving Gauss's law and removing gauge redundancies leaves degrees of freedom exactly on the two boundaries. They carry opposite-chirality lattice versions of level-$k$ $U(1)$ Kac-Moody current algebras. No Maxwell term is needed to generate the boundary phase space.

The compact and noncompact theories realize this structure differently. The noncompact theory has oscillator Fock spaces, whereas the compact finite lattice carries a finite Weyl algebra and finite-dimensional edge sectors. The lattice's prescription for attaching flux to charge also supplies a framing and reproduces the associated spin phase.

**Background.** The crucial boundary distinction is between gauge transformations that remain redundancies and transformations with nonzero boundary values, which act on physical edge variables. Symplectic reduction preserves the latter and their anomalous commutators. In the compact theory, exponentiated observables obey clock-and-shift relations; these admit finite representations even though continuum current-algebra modules are infinite-dimensional. Framing specifies a nearby displaced copy of a Wilson line, needed to define self-linking. Here that choice is built into the lattice flux-attachment rule.

**Why it matters?** This provides a concrete bulk-to-boundary derivation useful for lattice QFT and topological quantum matter. Continuous time and the precise boundary algebra are essential to interpreting its relation to lattice obstruction arguments. The result establishes boundary kinematics and anomaly structure; a particular propagating edge dispersion requires specifying boundary dynamics.

### Bounded decoding complexity in transversal circuits with magic state injection
**Lukas M. Sieberer, Giacomo Giudice — arXiv:2611.05502**

Transversal logical operations can reduce syndrome-extraction overhead, but they spread correlations through an entire computation. A decoder that accounts for those correlations appears to face a growing spacetime problem. This paper asks whether sequential processing can keep the relevant decoding volume bounded during a universal computation.

The authors decode logical measurement information in sequence, commit corrections, and remove syndrome information already consumed. In random Clifford circuits with a finite density of magic state injection, the decoding volume for an individual measurement approaches a stationary distribution. Its mean depends on injection density, while unusually large instances are exponentially suppressed. Measurements introduced by injection continually consume correlations that Clifford evolution creates.

The behavior persists across the studied connectivities and Clifford ensembles. Surprisingly, aggressively minimizing the number of measurements in a decoded product provides little benefit for the effective decoding volume. These are results about random-circuit decoding instances and their statistics, rather than a worst-case runtime theorem for arbitrary algorithms.

**Background.** An individual logical measurement can be intrinsically random while a product involving earlier outcomes is fixed by the initial stabilizers. Such a reliable measurement product supplies information that errors can corrupt and a decoder can restore. Backpropagating it identifies the relevant syndrome history. Sequential commitment prevents that history from being reused indefinitely. Magic state injection is important here because its intermediate measurements create opportunities to close off parts of the inference problem.

**Why it matters?** This identifies a favorable balance between universality and classical decoding cost in transversal architectures. The contribution is especially useful for deciding whether low syndrome overhead merely transfers an unacceptable burden to the decoder. Structured algorithms, rare large instances, and the suitability of fast graph-based decoders still need analysis before the observed bounded volumes imply an operational architecture.

**Thomas R. Scruby, Julio C. Magdalena de la Fuente — arXiv:2611.05867**

Assessing a fault-tolerant universal circuit should not require simulating the quantum algorithm it protects. Yet ordinary stabilizer simulation becomes difficult when physical non-Clifford gates appear. The authors exploit fault tolerance to separate the tractable question of whether decoding failed from the generally hard question of what the ideal computation outputs.

Their simulator propagates stochastic Pauli noise through Clifford blocks and suitable non-Clifford blocks containing T, controlled-S, and CCZ gates. A modified Pauli frame tracks error information without carrying the logical state. Nonlogical measurement statistics can be sampled independently of that state, allowing detector records to be generated and supplied to a decoder.

The approach requires high fault distance and the stated structure of logical blocks. Adaptive logical operations are supported when the controlling measurement distribution is known. A contractibility test certifies that tracked errors remain unable to affect logical information; approximate versions of that test can yield an upper bound on failure probability rather than an exact failure rate.

**Background.** A correctable error region cannot reveal the encoded state. Its Pauli frame must also avoid artificial logical flips introduced by ordinary reference-frame randomization. Success is determined from the residual logical action after decoding, without obtaining a noiseless logical reference outcome. Losing the correctability certificate triggers a failure declaration rather than an attempt to simulate the entire state.

**Why it matters?** This is a substantial methodological step toward benchmarking universal fault-tolerant circuits with classical resources. It complements decoding advances by making a broader class of circuit-level noise studies accessible. Its usefulness depends on efficient certification and decoding, and its conclusions concern the specified stochastic noise setting and supported gate blocks.

A subsystem can restore a symmetry during unitary evolution even when the whole state retains coherent information about its initial preparation. This paper constructs a two-copy measurement protocol and applies it to symmetry-restoration dynamics in the lattice Schwinger model.

The protocol combines a SWAP test with randomized symmetry rotations. It measures the purity of the subsystem both before and after removing coherence between charge sectors, then takes the logarithm of their ratio to obtain the second Rényi entanglement asymmetry. Classical state-vector calculations exhibit crossings of relaxation curves: some states starting with greater asymmetry restore symmetry sooner, giving a quantum Mpemba effect.

For additive precision $\epsilon$ on each measured purity, the shot count scales as $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})$ without explicit system-size dependence. That statement does not give a uniform cost for a fixed error in the final logarithmic ratio: small purities demand greater precision. **Background.** Charge dephasing keeps matrix elements within a charge sector and removes those connecting different sectors. A random average over suitably chosen symmetry rotations implements this channel without resolving or reconstructing the full density matrix. The SWAP test accesses purity by interfering two copies, avoiding tomography. The Mpemba comparison concerns the ordering of symmetry-restoration trajectories; greater initial symmetry breaking need not imply slower later relaxation. Finite-size residual asymmetry remains relevant in some tested initial-state families.

**Why it matters?** This connects a useful nonlinear diagnostic to an explicit measurement construction in a gauge-theory setting. ### Fermionic Porter-Thomas statistics and their emergence in random matchgate circuits
**Guglielmo Lami — arXiv:2611.05479**

Random-circuit benchmarks often compare measurement probabilities with the Porter-Thomas distribution of Haar-random states. Free-fermion circuits explore a much smaller, classically tractable state family, so the appropriate reference distribution is different. Lami derives that distribution and explains how local random matchgate circuits approach it.

For fermionic Gaussian states, both with and without particle-number conservation, rescaled probabilities have exact product distributions and become log-normal at large size. Their moments grow polynomially with mode count, producing weaker anticoncentration than ordinary Haar statistics. Clifford Gaussian states admit a related combinatorial description in terms of Majorana pairings and loops.

The same pairing picture exposes slow relaxation in one-dimensional brickwork circuits. A single-arc approximation captures the probability distribution's approach to equilibrium, with an early diffusive regime. For $L$ modes, the collision probability requires depth at least of order $L^2/\log^2 L$ to approach its asymptotic value within a fixed factor. Ideal generic free-fermion sampling also gives a linear cross-entropy score growing as $\sqrt{L}$, motivating a separately normalized logarithmic benchmark.

**Background.** Matchgates rotate Majorana operators linearly and preserve Gaussian states. Clifford matchgates specialize these rotations to signed permutations, so a state can be represented by paired Majoranas. Overlaying its pairing with the occupation-basis pairing forms loops that determine overlap probabilities. Tracking one arc reduces part of the many-pairing dynamics to a diffusion problem. The resulting statistics concern randomness within the Gaussian manifold; classical simulability is retained throughout.

**Why it matters?** This gives an exact reference ensemble for fermionic randomness and a physical explanation for its slow generation. It connects solvable circuit dynamics to benchmarking and makes large cross-entropy scores easier to interpret. Whether the proposed logarithmic score tracks fidelity under realistic noise beyond simple depolarization remains open.
