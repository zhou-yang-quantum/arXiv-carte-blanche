### A thermally and perturbatively stable classical spin model in two dimensions
**Shankar Balasubramanian, Margarita Davydova, Ting-Chun Lin, Ryan Thorngren — arXiv:2610.22548**

Can a two-dimensional local system remember information at nonzero temperature without requiring an exact protecting symmetry? An Ising ferromagnet illustrates the difficulty: thermal stability does not prevent a small symmetry-breaking field from favoring one stored value. A robust memory needs both a barrier against thermal errors and protection against local perturbations distinguishing its logical states.

The authors establish these properties for a previously constructed hierarchical classical code. Its recursive construction alternates two operations. Repetition refinements raise the energy barrier against errors, while parity refinements introduce transformations that change microscopic configurations without changing the stored information. Averaging over these transformations makes the logical states locally indistinguishable. Alternating the two refinements builds thermal and perturbative protection together while retaining geometric locality in two dimensions.

The theorem gives a memory lifetime satisfying $t_{\rm{mem}}\gtrsim\exp(N^{\alpha})$, where $N$ counts spins and $\alpha>0$, at sufficiently low nonzero temperature. The protection survives sufficiently weak local classical perturbations, with the allowed strength depending on temperature. Thus “arbitrary perturbations” concerns their form, within the stated locality and smallness conditions. The construction supports many metastable states and is analyzed under local stochastic thermal dynamics.

**Background.** The gauge transformations here are codeword changes that preserve the syndrome and logical value. Their supports occur at many length scales, so ordinary arguments involving only bounded-size gauge generators are insufficient. The proof performs gauge averaging one scale at a time, replacing a perturbation by an effective interaction obtained from its averaged Boltzmann weight. A cluster expansion organizes the logarithm of that average into connected contributions. The difficult step is keeping the effective interaction local through an increasing number of scales. Once this control is established, configurations near a decoding failure boundary have sufficiently small statistical weight to bound the escape rate from a memory sector.

**Why it matters?** This is a rigorous existence result for passive classical information storage with two distinct forms of stability in two dimensions. Its significance lies in how the hierarchy reconciles energetic confinement with local indistinguishability. For QEC and statistical mechanics, it offers a concrete example in which code structure organizes a stable dynamical memory. Understanding the necessary geometric complexity and obtaining useful finite-size lifetimes are the next questions for assessing its broader utility.

## arXiv-2026-10-19 — Item 2

### Topological Aspects of the $c=2$ Model in the Continuum and on the Lattice
**Yichul Choi, Nathan Seiberg, Sahand Seifnashri, Wucheng Zhang — arXiv:2610.22485**

A free-field theory can have globally subtle physics even when its local equations are simple. Here the puzzle is particularly concrete: a spatially varying coupling breaks ordinary translations, yet Fourier modes still decouple. What symmetry explains this solvability, and can a finite lattice retain the same structure?

The setting is the theory of two compact bosons whose target space is a torus. Besides its metric, the torus carries a periodic coupling $B$, analogous to a theta-angle. Letting this coupling wind around the spatial circle changes the global definition of the theory. Continuous target-space momentum symmetries are reduced to a projectively realized discrete subgroup. For a linear winding profile, some ordinary translations survive and combine nontrivially with internal symmetries; more general translations persist as non-invertible translation symmetries.

These generalized translations, together with the Hamiltonian’s locality and quadratic form, explain the separation of Fourier modes. The authors then construct a modified Villain formulation on a finite lattice that reproduces the corresponding symmetry algebra, including the effects of the winding coupling. The lattice realization therefore retains topological information that a naive discretization could obscure.

**Background.** The identification $B\sim B+1$ means that the coupling itself takes values on a circle. A spatial profile can wind around that circle even though it cannot be represented by one globally periodic real-valued function. Calling this a minus-one-form symmetry packages the periodic coupling into the language of background fields. Its transformations mix with the backgrounds for ordinary symmetries, producing a higher-group structure: the transformations cannot all be specified independently.

A non-invertible translation includes a projection onto allowed winding sectors. It commutes with the Hamiltonian but annihilates some states, so no inverse translation can undo it on the entire Hilbert space. This still supplies useful constraints on the dynamics. In the lattice construction, integer link variables keep track of compact-field winding, while gauge constraints identify redundant descriptions. Those extra variables preserve the global sectors needed for the continuum symmetry algebra.

**Why it matters?** The paper makes generalized symmetry operational in an explicitly solvable continuum–lattice pair. Its strongest lesson is that the absence of an ordinary translation group need not remove the symmetry constraints underlying mode decomposition. This is valuable for QFT and exactly solvable models because it connects global topology, spectral organization, and a regulator that preserves their relationship. Extending such control to interacting models would test how much of the solvability mechanism survives beyond quadratic dynamics.

### Nonergodicity from Hilbert-Subspace Cloning
**Yuuya Chiba, Ryusuke Hamazaki — arXiv:2610.23105**

Chaotic dynamics usually erases locally accessible information about the initial state. Spectral degeneracy creates a possible exception: states with exactly the same energy do not acquire relative dynamical phases. The question is whether an exponentially large degeneracy can preserve observable quantum coherence while the remaining degrees of freedom behave chaotically.

The authors construct spin Hamiltonians from weighted translates of a single Pauli string. Spatial reversal of the string generates local conserved operators that commute with the Hamiltonian terms but need not commute with one another. This algebra forces a precise decomposition: within each global symmetry sector, the Hamiltonian acts on an active factor and as the identity on an exponentially large second factor. Equivalently, the same active Hamiltonian appears in exponentially many disconnected copies.

The decomposition is exact. Numerical level statistics and eigenstate tests support chaotic behavior within the active blocks. Assuming eigenstate thermalization there, the authors derive a partial Gibbs ensemble for long-time local observables: the active degrees of freedom thermalize, while the second factor retains its initial quantum state. Some of this coherence is detectable by local physical observables. The thermalization statement therefore has an explicit dynamical assumption beyond the exact algebraic construction.

**Background.** Noncommuting conserved quantities cannot all be treated as simultaneous classical sector labels. Their algebra can instead act on a genuine quantum subsystem. An operator commuting with that entire algebra must act trivially on the associated factor, which explains the identity in the Hamiltonian decomposition.

The distinction between identical copies and merely disconnected sectors is essential. If different sectors had different spectra, coherence between them could dephase. Here corresponding states in different copies have identical energies, so their relative phase is preserved. Physical local operators can have components acting on the protected factor, making this retained information observable without reconstructing the whole many-body state. “Cloning” names the repeated Hamiltonian blocks; it does not describe copying an unknown quantum state.

**Why it matters?** This provides a sharp mechanism for quantum memory within otherwise chaotic many-body dynamics. It shows why a large noncommuting conservation algebra requires more than an ensemble fixing classical charge values. The connection to non-equilibrium physics is substantive: local relaxation can coexist with a protected quantum subsystem. Its usefulness as a memory principle will depend on what remains under perturbations that weaken the exact algebraic structure.

### Complete $g^6$ pressure of hot Yang-Mills theory
**Keijo Kajantie, Pablo Navarrete, Risto Paatelainen, Leon Sandbote, York Schröder, Kaapo Seppänen, Aleksi Vuorinen — arXiv:2610.23675**

Even at high temperature, where the gauge coupling is small, a non-Abelian plasma contains long-distance magnetic fluctuations that remain nonperturbative. Computing its pressure therefore requires a controlled assembly of short-distance perturbation theory and lower-dimensional effective theories. At sixth order in the coupling, the missing short-distance contribution had prevented completion of this program for more than two decades.

The authors calculate that contribution by evaluating nine previously unknown four-loop thermal sum-integrals. Thermal loop-tree duality performs the Matsubara frequency sums analytically, leaving spatial-momentum integrals. Local ultraviolet and infrared subtractions isolate singular pieces for analytic treatment and leave finite remainders suitable for numerical integration. This supplies the hard-scale coefficient for general SU(N) pure gauge theory.

Combining it with previously known electric and nonperturbative magnetic contributions completes the pressure through this order for SU(3). The calculation explicitly verifies cancellation of infrared divergences between the different scales. With the authors’ effective-theory resummation, the pressure agrees with four-dimensional lattice data down to temperatures a few times the deconfinement temperature. The numerical evaluation has an uncertainty, and the complete result retains uncertainty from the earlier magnetic input.

**Background.** The relevant momentum scales are $2\pi T$, $gT$, and $g^{2}T$, with T the temperature and g the gauge coupling. They describe thermal modes, electrostatic screening, and magnetostatic fluctuations, respectively. Dimensional reduction integrates out the first two scales successively, leaving three-dimensional theories for the slower fields.

At the magnetic scale, additional loops are no longer parametrically suppressed. A finite-order four-dimensional calculation cannot determine that contribution by itself. The effective-theory construction isolates it in a separate nonperturbative constant. Matching is what makes the pieces meaningful together: divergences and auxiliary scale dependence cancel when the hard, electric, and magnetic contributions are combined. Completing the hard coefficient consequently tests the separation of scales as well as supplying a missing number.

**Why it matters?** This closes a major quantitative gap in thermal QFT at the first pressure order receiving contributions from all three scales. The lattice comparison gives the completed construction a demanding independent benchmark. For broader QFT interests, the reusable advance is the combination of thermal integral representations with local subtraction methods. Extending the calculation to dynamical quarks would be necessary before drawing corresponding quantitative conclusions for full QCD.

### Area Theorems for Quantum Error Correction
**Avijit Mandal, Mert Gökduman, Henry D. Pfister — arXiv:2610.22710**

A decoding threshold describes where logical information becomes unrecoverable, but it does not reveal all the constraints on how uncertainty accumulates as noise increases. Classical coding theory has area theorems connecting an entire information-response curve to the code rate. Extending them to quantum codes requires tracking logical correction classes, because identifying the physical error more precisely than its stabilizer equivalence class is unnecessary.

The authors formulate a classical coset-decoding problem and prove that its conditional entropy reproduces the logical correction uncertainty of a stabilizer code. Differentiating this uncertainty with respect to the channel-noise parameter defines the quantum generalized extrinsic information transfer, or GEXIT, function. With the appropriate normalization, its integrated area equals the code rate $k/n$.

For depolarizing noise, the construction retains the joint four-valued Pauli-error distribution, including correlations between bit and phase components. For CSS codes, erasures and independent bit- and phase-flip noise permit a reduction to two binary coset problems. On the quantum erasure channel, a duality relation at complementary erasure probabilities combines with the area theorem to recover a rate bound matching the channel capacity. A separate argument relates correction-class entropy to coherent information and gives a depolarizing-channel converse.

**Background.** An EXIT-type quantity measures the increase in decoding uncertainty caused by worsening a local channel while retaining information supplied by the rest of the block. Summing these coordinate responses gives the derivative of the total conditional entropy. Integrating the derivative then fixes the area from the two endpoint entropies.

The quantum normalization matters. At fully depolarizing noise, each encoded qubit has two bits of unresolved logical Pauli information. Dividing the entropy derivative by twice the physical block length makes the endpoint change produce the quantum rate. The nontrivial work is identifying the correct coset entropy and proving the response bounds and duality relations needed to turn this identity into coding constraints.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a general constraint on optimal stabilizer decoding across a whole noise trajectory. It could help organize threshold proofs and comparisons between code families by relating their rates to the shape of logical uncertainty. For the statistical-mechanics perspective on QEC, the entropy derivative is a natural response observable. Extracting a sharp threshold for a particular family still requires control of that response; the fixed area alone does not determine its shape.

### Transport and thermalization under noncommuting localized constraints
**Amir Nesturov, Attila Takács, Lenart Zadnik — arXiv:2610.23781**

If local conservation laws divide a system into exponentially many inaccessible sectors, must energy transport also become anomalous? Fragmentation restricts which states are reachable, but energy can still move efficiently within each accessible sector. Separating these questions requires models where the internal dynamics of the fragments can be understood explicitly.

The authors study the quantum Volterra model, a nonintegrable classical limit, and two related cellular automata. In the discrete systems, localized constraints produce fragments of equal size. For the quantum model, a local change of basis maps each fragment to a chiral Potts chain with sector-dependent phases. Under stated system-size conditions, further on-site transformations make these chains homogeneous.

The quantum energy current is conserved, giving ballistic transport; the infinite-temperature transport analysis yields the same behavior across fragments. Numerical studies of the nonintegrable classical system instead find diffusion, with the same diffusion constant whether the localized constraints are fixed or averaged over. The cellular automata show strong failure of local ergodicity even though the constraints leave transport unaffected. These examples separate transport behavior from the mere existence of disconnected state-space components.

**Background.** The constraints commute with the Hamiltonian but not generally with one another. To label fragments, one resolves a maximal commuting subset and then identifies the remaining dynamical degrees of freedom. The resulting three-state clock variables form the Potts description. Their shift and phase operators are finite-dimensional analogues of conjugate variables; complex phases in the couplings give the chiral structure.

Uniform Hilbert-space fragmentation removes one common complication: different initial sectors do not have wildly different dimensions. It does not make their transport properties identical automatically. Here that conclusion uses the explicit sector mapping and the current structure. In the classical limit, the corresponding conserved quantities restrict trajectories to invariant submanifolds. Their algebra also distinguishes this construction from ordinary local gauge constraints.

**Why it matters?** The useful conceptual result is a controlled separation between inaccessible information and mobile energy. A system can retain local memory without its energy spreading becoming slow or sector dependent. This sharpens the interpretation of non-equilibrium diagnostics and supplies an exact bridge to familiar integrable chains. Read alongside Item 3, it also shows why the algebra and dynamics inside conserved sectors matter more than counting sectors alone.

### Impact of Irrep Truncations on Quantum Simulations of QCD
**Patrick Draper, Drishti Gupta — arXiv:2610.23681**

A quantum computer cannot store the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space carried by a continuous gauge field on each lattice link. Keeping only a finite set of electric-flux representations preserves gauge structure naturally, but its physical adequacy becomes uncertain toward weak coupling. Resource estimates therefore need evidence about which representations the vacuum actually occupies.

The authors develop observables that measure relative link-representation probabilities in the ground state of pure SU(3) lattice gauge theory. They also probe site singlets: the distinct ways incoming and outgoing flux representations can combine into a gauge-invariant state at a vertex. The method inserts seams into an anisotropic Euclidean lattice, connecting Monte Carlo measurements to electric-basis probabilities in the Hamiltonian description.

They compare two lattice actions and extrapolate toward the Hamiltonian limit, alongside weak-coupling perturbative calculations. At $\beta=6.0$, the measured suppression of higher representations motivates the authors’ suggested truncation $p+q\leq 3$. This is evidence at the studied coupling and volumes, rather than a general error bound for real-time simulation. Leading-order perturbation theory also retains a significant quantitative discrepancy with the lattice measurements after coupling improvements.

**Background.** An SU(3) representation is labeled by two nonnegative integers $(p,q)$; these specify how its states transform, rather than simply giving the representation’s dimension. A cutoff on their sum limits the retained electric-flux content. At a vertex, even a fixed collection of link representations can admit multiple singlets, so link probabilities alone do not completely characterize a gauge-invariant truncation.

The seam construction addresses a mismatch of bases. Ordinary Euclidean sampling uses group-valued link configurations, while electric-flux projectors are not diagonal in that basis. Doubling selected link variables across a time slice allows insertion of group characters—traces of representation matrices—that select the desired flux content. Taking temporal lattice spacing small relative to spatial spacing then connects these measurements to the lattice Hamiltonian vacuum.

**Why it matters?** This makes truncation design an empirical field-theory calculation instead of a largely assumed hardware input. Its value for quantum simulation is the route from accessible classical vacuum data to choices of encoded Hilbert space. A convincing resource prescription will additionally require control of omitted tails, larger-volume behavior, and the representations populated during the particular dynamics being simulated. The current work provides tools for investigating those questions.

### Inducing Extraordinary-log Criticality through a Quantum Spin Chain
**Grigorios Makris, Francesco Parisen Toldin, Stefan Wessel — arXiv:2610.23221**

Coupling a quantum chain to a critical environment can change its long-distance behavior more radically than simply renormalizing its parameters. One unusual possibility is a state whose correlations decay as a power of a logarithm of distance. Establishing this extraordinary-log criticality in a microscopic quantum system requires distinguishing very slow running from ordinary power-law scaling and finite-size crossover.

The authors couple a spin-half Heisenberg chain to a two-dimensional Heisenberg bilayer tuned to its quantum critical point. Under the quantum-to-classical mapping, the chain becomes a plane defect in a three-dimensional critical system. Sign-problem-free quantum Monte Carlo simulations probe its effective coupling through the appropriately size-rescaled spin stiffness.

The data support logarithmic stiffness growth and a running coupling $g(L)\sim 1/\log L$, where L is linear system size. More discriminatingly, the measured step-scaling function follows the predicted flow including leading and subleading universal coefficients. The principal coupling geometry avoids a strong local-singlet tendency that complicates a simpler single-bond attachment. The evidence supports the proposed universality class despite the topological term inherited from the isolated spin-half chain.

**Background.** In the defect’s nonlinear sigma model, the effective coupling measures fluctuations of the spin orientation and is inversely related to a dimensionless stiffness. The critical environment changes its renormalization flow so that weak coupling becomes stable. Fluctuations then weaken only logarithmically with distance, producing correlations that decay much more slowly than a conventional critical power law.

A step-scaling function compares the effective coupling at one system size with its value after doubling that size. This directly tests the flow rather than relying only on a fit to a slowly varying observable. The chain’s topological theta-term assigns phases to distinct spin configurations; its presence makes agreement with the corresponding classical defect flow a substantive test of the quantum realization.

**Why it matters?** This is quantitative microscopic evidence for an unconventional defect universality class in a quantum many-body system. The agreement of the flow, rather than just one logarithmic trend, strengthens the identification. For QFT and quantum criticality, the model provides a useful setting for studying how a gapless environment reshapes a lower-dimensional theory while retaining its quantum topological structure.

### Anyonic Neural Network Wavefunctions
**Andres Perez Fadon, Pok Man Tam, Leyna Shackleton — arXiv:2610.23815**

An ideal gas of Abelian anyons is already a difficult many-body problem: exchange statistics creates correlations even without an ordinary interaction potential. Numerical progress has been restricted by small accessible particle numbers and by the singular behavior of the wavefunction when particles approach one another. A flexible variational state must capture that behavior without imposing incorrect constraints on larger particle clusters.

The authors introduce AnyNet, a neural wavefunction architecture that builds the required pair-coincidence behavior analytically into a determinant-based ansatz. Simply multiplying a generic network by a pairwise Jastrow factor can fail: imposing the right behavior separately for every pair can force an incorrect rate of vanishing when three particles coalesce. AnyNet instead modifies the orbital columns entering its determinants, allowing the relevant short-distance channels while retaining more general many-body structure.

The method agrees well with exact diagonalization for four anyons, reaches sixteen particles at intermediate statistics, and treats twenty near the fermionic limit, where comparison with perturbation theory gives a discrepancy within 0.4%. Applications include a harmonic trap and an impurity-binding problem. The calculations examine densities, angular momentum, and multiparticle coalescence; they extend the numerical reach without yet settling the thermodynamic phase structure.

**Background.** Statistical transmutation trades a multivalued anyonic wavefunction for a single-valued fermionic wavefunction coupled to a statistical gauge field. The exchange phase is handled explicitly, but the transformed kinetic energy contains singular statistical interactions. This is why an apparently free anyon problem still demands a strongly correlated variational state.

The paper chooses hard-core anyons, whose wavefunction vanishes at coincidence for nonzero statistics parameter. Correct short-distance powers help ensure that applying the singular Hamiltonian does not produce uncontrollable local-energy fluctuations. In AnyNet, two approaching determinant columns develop a prescribed difference, setting the pairwise vanishing behavior. The remaining learned structure can still organize configurations involving three or more nearby particles, where a rigid product of pair factors can be too restrictive.

**Why it matters?** The methodological advance is to encode a singular Hamiltonian’s local domain into a flexible many-body ansatz. This is more informative than increasing network size alone: it identifies a physical obstruction and changes the representation to remove it. For quantum simulation and many-body theory, the resulting larger anyon calculations offer new benchmarks and a route toward testing longstanding predictions about statistical interactions. Thermodynamic conclusions will require substantially larger systems and further convergence analysis.

### Regularization of stochastic fluid dynamics and non-perturbative renormalization of the shear viscosity of incompressible fluids
**Mattis Harhoff, Cedric Riedel, Johannes Roth, Sören Schlichting — arXiv:2610.23442**

Thermal fluctuations alter the viscosity of a fluid, but treating hydrodynamic noise as white down to arbitrarily short distances introduces ultraviolet sensitivity. If numerical lattice spacing also supplies the physical cutoff, refining a simulation changes the effective theory being simulated. A controlled comparison between continuum calculations and lattice dynamics needs these two scales separated.

The authors introduce ultraviolet regularizations of incompressible stochastic hydrodynamics that preserve its conservation laws and equilibrium structure. One option modifies the high-momentum viscous response together with its associated noise. The resulting theory admits a continuum discretization limit $\Lambda a\to 0$ at fixed physical cutoff $\Lambda$, where $a$ is the numerical lattice spacing.

They derive renormalization group improved viscosity calculations and compare them with direct stochastic simulations in two and three dimensions. The results reproduce logarithmic growth toward long wavelengths in two dimensions and a finite infinite-volume viscosity in three dimensions. In the latter case, simulations exhibit a minimum renormalized viscosity as the bare viscosity is varied. A self-consistent improvement also captures the subsequent rise at small bare viscosity substantially better than the elementary one-loop approximation.

**Background.** The fluctuation–dissipation relation ties thermal noise to dissipative response. Changing the viscous kernel while leaving the noise untouched would generally change the equilibrium distribution. The regularization therefore modifies these ingredients together; when the Hamiltonian is regulated, the reversible dynamics must also be adjusted consistently. This preserves the structural relations needed for meaningful continuum and numerical comparisons.

Renormalization group improvement accounts for feedback between fluctuations and viscosity: fluctuations change the viscosity, and the changed viscosity alters the fluctuations still to be integrated out. The physical ultraviolet cutoff remains part of this effective description. Taking numerical lattice spacing to zero does not remove the microscopic boundary of hydrodynamics. Correspondingly, the observed viscosity minimum depends on that cutoff and can be interpreted as a limit on how far a hydrodynamic description can extend for a fixed measured viscosity.

**Why it matters?** This establishes a common regulated framework for analytic and numerical studies of fluctuating transport. Its significance for non-equilibrium field theory is the ability to distinguish physical fluctuation effects from discretization artifacts while preserving conservation and equilibrium constraints. Matching the regulator-dependent bare parameters to an underlying microscopic theory remains necessary for cutoff-independent predictions about a particular fluid.
