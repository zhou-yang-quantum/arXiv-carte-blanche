## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 1

### The Clifford Hierarchy from Clifford Stabilizers in 2D
**Alison Warman, Sakura Schafer-Nameki — arXiv:2611.25025**

How much of the difficulty of a logical gate must already be present in the physical operations that implement it? The Bravyi–König theorem answers this sharply for two-dimensional topological Pauli stabilizer codes, restricting constant-depth logical gates to Clifford operations. Warman and Schafer-Nameki show that replacing Pauli constraints by Clifford constraints changes the answer much more dramatically than previously conjectured.

They construct non-Abelian lattice gauge codes encoding $k$ logical qubits with a transversal $C^{k-1}Z$ gate: its phase is $(-1)^{x_1x_2\cdots x_k}$ on a logical computational-basis state. Although this reaches arbitrary levels of the Clifford hierarchy as the construction grows, the physical gate layer uses only $Z$ or $CZ$. The mechanism is an encoding in which noncommuting flux multiplication forces a binary flux to record the product of all the logical bits. A simple physical phase then reads out that nonlinear dependence. The codes retain distance growing with patch size, while the number of physical qubits per lattice edge grows with the hierarchy level.

**Background.** A quantum double is a lattice gauge model for a finite group: edge variables carry group elements, and its excitations include magnetic fluxes and electric charges. Boundaries can absorb selected fluxes, allowing flux configurations connecting different boundaries to encode information. For a non-Abelian group, crossing and combining fluxes changes their labels in an order-dependent way. Here that algebra supplies the logical nonlinearity before the gate is applied. This differs fundamentally from the linear binary structure behind ordinary Pauli stabilizer encodings; the familiar two-dimensional gate restriction therefore does not apply to these codes.

**Why it matters?** This is a substantial conceptual advance in the relation between topological order and fault-tolerant logic. It shows that stabilizer complexity alone does not bound logical gate complexity in the proposed way. Turning that observation into a competitive computer will require preparation, syndrome extraction, and decoding of the enlarged non-Abelian codes; the simple gate layer establishes an opportunity rather than a complete overhead comparison.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 2

### High-temperature Gibbs states are uniformly Markovian and trivial
**Andrew D. Kim, Tai Hsuan Yang, Jong Yeon Lee — arXiv:2611.26626**

A hot quantum system should have little long-range information, but making that intuition strong enough for mixed-state phase classification has been surprisingly difficult. In particular, conditional mutual information must remain controlled when part of the environment is discarded. Bounds that require the conditioning region to fill the entire gap do not establish this stronger property.

The authors prove exponential decay of mutual information and conditional mutual information for finite-range, bounded-strength qudit Hamiltonians above a system-size-independent temperature threshold, including noncommuting interactions. For arbitrary disjoint regions, their conditional bound has the form
$$I(A:C|B)\leq K\min\{|\partial A|,|\partial C|\}e^{-r/R}.$$
Here r is the separation of A and C, R is the interaction range, and K is independent of system size. Other sites can be traced out. The proof represents the Gibbs state as a positive mixture of products of local projectors drawn from projective 2-designs, and controls the resulting scalar weights through a convergent cluster expansion.

**Background.** The useful feature of the overcomplete projector representation is its compatibility with taking marginals: discarding a qudit becomes averaging its classical label. Ordinary expansions of the logarithm of a reduced quantum Gibbs state encounter noncommutativity problems. Moving the expansion to scalar weights avoids that obstruction. The conditional-information argument additionally controls how local resampling of labels erases distant influence while retaining a quantum conditioning system; separability by itself would not establish the required conditional bound.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a rigorous foundation for treating sufficiently hot quantum Gibbs states as a trivial mixed-state phase. The advance is the uniformity under discarding subsystems, a property directly relevant to local recovery and information-based phase definitions. It also gives a valuable reference regime against which noise-induced or finite-temperature information transitions can be distinguished. The theorem concerns a controlled high-temperature region, rather than the whole thermodynamically disordered phase.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 3

### Local-channel inequivalence within analytic symmetry-broken phases
**Guanxi Xun, Rintaro Masaoka, Weiguang Cao, Haruki Watanabe — arXiv:2611.26139**

Does changing temperature smoothly within an ordered phase preserve the state’s phase under local quantum operations? Analytic free energy and an unchanged broken symmetry might suggest that it does. This paper finds an obstruction: colder states can share more accurately readable information about their common order direction.

The authors establish the separation for symmetry-invariant Gibbs states of a fixed-density ideal Bose gas and a vortex-free Villain model in $d\geq3$. Two distant observers optimize measurements on large regions. Their accessible mutual information has a temperature-independent leading logarithm, but its constant contains
$\mathcal{C}(T)=\tfrac12\ln[\Upsilon(T)/T]+c_{\mathrm{geom}}$,
where the phase stiffness is denoted by Upsilon and the remaining constant depends on geometry. This quantity increases upon cooling and cannot increase under the allowed quasi-local channels. Distinct ordered temperatures therefore fail two-way channel equivalence. Finite-system cooling with vanishing error requires a causal radius at least proportional to system size.

**Background.** A symmetric ordered state averages over possible order directions. Far-separated regions share that direction even though each has its own thermal fluctuations. Averaging measurements over a larger block improves the angular estimate; the stiffness determines the cost of slowly varying that angle and hence the remaining uncertainty. A local conversion would let an observer simulate a cold measurement from a slightly enlarged warm region. That enlargement cannot supply the finite improvement in shared angular precision required by the colder state. The distinction survives in the subleading information constant, even though the leading scaling agrees.

**Why it matters?** The result exposes a meaningful difference between thermodynamic and operational notions of mixed-state phases. It suggests that information stored in Goldstone fluctuations can distinguish states throughout one ordered regime. The proven examples have unbounded local spaces or a vortex-excluding constraint, so extension to ordinary finite-dimensional interacting magnets is a substantive next question. For information-based phase classification, these are useful counterexamples to assuming that thermodynamic smoothness guarantees local convertibility.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 4

### Certified Discovery of Quantum Phase Diagrams
**Yuanxi Liu, John Preskill, Hsin-Yuan Huang — arXiv:2611.25064**

A quantum simulator can prepare states that are hard to verify classically, but a phase diagram extracted from them still needs evidence that its reported boundaries are real. Looking for a guessed order parameter is inadequate when the phases themselves are unknown. This paper develops an algorithm that explores parameter space while producing measured certificates of small gaps.

Starting from reference preparations, the algorithm transports candidate ground states along gapped paths and tests their low-frequency response to local probes. Excitation weight below a chosen cutoff certifies a gap below that resolution. The Hamiltonian gradient supplies a suitable probe: for transitions whose finite-size gap closes no faster than inverse polynomially, the authors establish that the transition cannot remain invisible to this response. Energy comparisons help select among competing transported preparations.

The quantum procedure has polynomial cost at inverse-polynomial gap resolution, provided successful reference preparations cover every resolved gapped region. Exponentially small avoided crossings remain outside that general guarantee. Gentle measurements allow reuse of a single reference copy in the unique-ground-state case.

**Background.** Quasi-adiabatic continuation transports a ground-state subspace using a filtered generator constructed from the changing Hamiltonian. Its locality depends on maintaining a gap. The key logical bridge is that a genuinely invisible gap closing would also allow the filtered transport to cross without appreciably changing the state’s phase. That contradicts the intended phase distinction. Frequency filtering therefore connects a dynamical measurement to the obstruction to local ground-state transport; it is more informative than observing that one conventional correlation function changes.

**Why it matters?** This is an important algorithmic route from quantum simulation to certified physical inference. Its strength is learning boundaries without phase labels or prescribed order parameters. The seed-coverage and gap assumptions identify exactly where physical input remains essential: it does not solve arbitrary ground-state preparation or guarantee discovery of an entirely unseeded region. Within those assumptions, it provides a useful framework for designing trustworthy simulator experiments.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 5

### Algorithmic Quantum Sensing
**Ishaan Kannan, Aditi Venkatesh, Jordan Cotler — arXiv:2611.25572**

Sensing a broadband time-dependent field is a learning problem: resolving one known tone, reconstructing a spectrum, and estimating power in a band demand different information. A pulse sequence optimized for one task need not be optimal for another. Kannan, Venkatesh, and Cotler organize these tasks into a common framework and ask what changes when a sensor can use quantum computation.

They first derive task-dependent pulse protocols using conventional sensor control, then introduce quantum computational spectroscopy. Its central construction transfers the field’s Fourier information into coherent phases of a memory register. A quantum algorithm subsequently extracts the desired information from that register. For normalized linear spectroscopy with N frequencies and additive precision epsilon, the interrogation time reaches $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{N}/\epsilon)$. In the relevant broadband regimes this surpasses the conventional AC sensing bounds, and the separation can persist under constant-strength sensor noise.

**Background.** A phase oracle applies a phase depending on an unknown function to a superposition of inputs. Here the unknown function is supplied by a physical signal rather than a digital database. Memory-controlled sensor rotations write different combinations of Fourier amplitudes into different branches, after which coherent processing can access many combinations together. The noise protection uses rotations that are nearly identical across branches: a sensor error then reveals little about which memory branch was occupied. This protects the information needed for the algorithmic gain.

**Why it matters?** This is a broad proposal for quantum algorithms whose input is an analog physical field. The headline “beyond Heisenberg” statement concerns an enlarged computational resource model for broadband learning; it should not be read as an unrestricted improvement to single-parameter metrology. Its potential impact depends on memory quality and control overhead, since the asymptotic interrogation-time advantages assume fast control. The conventional pulse protocols also offer a more immediate route to testing the framework.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 6

### Field theory and the biquadratic spin-1 chain
**Luisa Eck, Paul Fendley — arXiv:2611.26610**

The antiferromagnetic biquadratic spin-1 chain presents a puzzle for continuum reasoning. It is integrable and has $SU(3)$ symmetry, while its usual candidate field theory, the $\mathbb{CP}^2$ sigma model at $\theta=\pi$, is generally believed not to be integrable. Matching symmetries and low-energy particles does not by itself resolve that discrepancy.

Eck and Fendley use the Bethe ansatz to identify deconfined domain-wall excitations and construct a different scaling route. They add a higher conserved charge that preserves the chain’s symmetries and integrability. Since the added charge commutes with the original Hamiltonian, it changes excitation energies without changing eigenstates. At the tuned transition, the gap closes with quadratic dispersion. The resulting continuum limit has $z=2$ and is nonrelativistic. They characterize its two-particle scattering using the symmetry structure and integrability, with Galilean invariance as an assumption.

**Background.** The two dimerized vacua allow a domain wall to separate regions of equal bulk energy, so separating walls does not generate a confining energy cost. Oppositely oriented walls carry the three-dimensional fundamental representation and its conjugate. A wall–antiwall pair can combine into a symmetry singlet or an eight-dimensional adjoint multiplet, giving distinct scattering channels. Adding a conserved charge retains this particle organization while changing the dispersion. Thus the choice of how the lattice gap is closed determines which spacetime symmetry survives in the continuum.

**Why it matters?** This clarifies how an integrable lattice model can resemble a familiar relativistic field theory without supplying its integrable regularization. The constructive contribution is a symmetry-preserving route to a different, nonrelativistic scattering theory. It is especially interesting as an example of higher-charge deformations selecting new continuum dynamics. A full field-theory formulation and further tests of the proposed scattering description would strengthen that identification.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 7

### When the Noise Gets Noisy: Quantum Error Correction with Disordered Noise and Misinformed Decoding
**Cory T. Aitchison, Lucas H. English, Benjamin Béri, Dominic J. Williamson, Campbell K. McLauchlan — arXiv:2611.26506**

Two devices with the same mean physical error rate need not be equally correctable. Spatial variation and temporal drift change which faults are likely, while calibration determines how much of that structure a decoder can exploit. This paper asks which features of a noise distribution predict logical performance.

Treating local error probabilities as random variables, the authors distinguish correctly characterized, uncharacterized, and mischaracterized decoding. Replica arguments predict that characterized maximum-likelihood thresholds are approximately organized by average channel entropy; surface-code numerics support this picture across several noise settings. Deep below threshold, averages of the Bhattacharyya coefficient bound the logical-error decay rate for matching-based decoding. Incorrect decoder weights bring in cross entropy and a corresponding mismatched overlap measure. Merely knowing the distribution, without its individual realizations, generally loses the advantage available from characterization.

**Background.** There are two levels of randomness: the local probability is drawn, and then a fault occurs according to that probability. Averaging probabilities before decoding erases information that would distinguish reliable locations from unreliable ones. For a binary mechanism, $B(p)=2\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ measures overlap between complementary fault hypotheses. Products of these overlaps control how easily an error chain can be confused with a competing chain. Cross entropy instead quantifies the logarithmic penalty for assigning probabilities different from the true ones. These quantities therefore probe different aspects of the decoding problem.

**Why it matters?** The paper gives a useful statistical-mechanical language for deciding what calibration is worth learning and how much inaccurate calibration can cost. Its practical message is more informative than replacing an inhomogeneous device by one average error rate. The entropic threshold criterion remains an approximate prediction with numerical support, while the subthreshold bounds have more specific assumptions. Extending this organization to stronger correlations and coherent noise would be particularly valuable.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 8

### Twist and Shout: defect lines on the R\'enyi torus
**Naveen Balaji Umasankar — arXiv:2611.25032**

Ordinary interval entropies reveal only a limited part of a critical theory’s structure. Can an entanglement construction distinguish symmetries that have identical fusion rules but different anomaly data? This paper inserts topological defect lines into the two-interval replica geometry to expose that extra information.

For the second Rényi moment, the replica surface is a torus. Wrapping a line around one interval defines a cross-ratio-dependent observable. For an on-site group symmetry it is the normalized overlap
$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{AB}g_A\rho_{AB}g_A^{-1})/\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{AB}^2)$.
The adjacent-interval asymptotics depend on the lightest operator on which the defect can end. Analytic continuation around the interval-collision point probes the allowed spins of those operators, which encode anomaly information. The author evaluates examples with ordinary and non-invertible lines and checks the Ising spin-flip observable against a lattice calculation.

**Background.** A topological line commutes with the stress tensor and can be moved without changing a correlator unless it crosses an insertion. Non-invertible lines generalize group symmetries: composing two can produce a sum of lines rather than a single reversible operation. Their fusion rules specify which lines appear, but associativity data specify how different orders of fusion are related. Exchanging the torus cycles turns a line insertion into a defect Hilbert space. Fractional spins in that space determine the observable’s monodromy, meaning its change under analytic continuation around a singular point.

**Why it matters?** This connects accessible reduced-state constructions with modern generalized symmetry data. The conceptual gain is sensitivity beyond fusion rules, while the exact Ising calculation provides a concrete bridge to lattice entanglement. The scope is the second Rényi construction, and anomaly extraction uses analytic structure rather than just one real-geometry measurement. Recovering an entire fusion category from higher-genus entanglement remains a proposed broader direction.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 9

### Wigner-Dyson correlations in selected sectors of an integrable spin chain
**Balázs Pozsgay — arXiv:2611.26263**

Level repulsion is commonly treated as evidence for quantum chaos after ordinary symmetries have been resolved. How reliable is that inference when conserved structure is highly nonlocal? Pozsgay constructs a clean nearest-neighbor integrable chain in which selected periodic-chain sectors nevertheless show Wigner–Dyson-like correlations.

Each site carries a binary label and a color qubit. A nonlocal change of basis maps the open chain to the isotropic Heisenberg chain acting on labels, with the colors supplying exponentially many identical copies. Closing the chain leaves a color-dependent boundary twist and splits those degeneracies. In a sector with a single B label and fixed momentum, each invariant color channel contributes only one energy. Numerically, correlations among these energies resemble the unitary random-matrix class. Half-filled sectors instead exhibit approximately Poisson statistics in the reported tests.

**Background.** Hilbert-space fragmentation here means that the algebra commuting with every local interaction has exponentially growing dimension. Its conserved operators need not be local or obvious from on-site symmetries. The residual boundary twist records the cumulative color transformation acquired when a label travels around the ring. Consequently, resolving particle number and physical momentum still leaves many invariant fragments. Correlations between their eigenvalues do not imply that time evolution mixes the fragments.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful counterexample to identifying spectral statistics with dynamical chaos, with an unusually explicit mechanism for the mismatch. Its value for nonequilibrium physics is diagnostic: apparently chaotic spacing statistics can coexist with exact integrable organization hidden by a nonlocal encoding. The numerical observation concerns selected sectors and correlations across fragments; a broader claim of chaotic dynamics would require independent dynamical evidence.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 10

### Exact finite-time fluctuation-response relations for Markov processes: A martingale framework
**Tan Van Vu, Van Tuan Vo, Ruicheng Bao, Keiji Saito — arXiv:2611.26267**

How much dissipation can be inferred from the fluctuations and response of a measured current before a system reaches its long-time regime? Standard uncertainty relations can obscure the role of preparation, and familiar overdamped formulas can fail badly when inertia matters. This paper develops identities that separate these contributions from the outset.

The central construction replaces a time-integrated observable by its conditional expected final value given the trajectory observed so far. This produces a Doob martingale. Its fluctuations isolate noise generated during observation from variability inherited from the initial ensemble. Responses are expressed through covariance with a martingale representing the perturbation, producing exact identities before inequalities are applied.

For thermal underdamped dynamics, changing friction at fixed temperature gives a finite-time uncertainty relation,
$$V_{\mathrm{dyn}}\Sigma_\tau\geq2R_\gamma^2.$$
The left-hand factors are the noise-generated variance and entropy production; the right-hand side is twice the squared response to a fractional friction change at fixed preparation. A saturating observable exists within the paper’s full observable class.

**Background.** A martingale has no predictable change conditioned on its past. An accumulated current generally does have drift, but adding its expected future contribution cancels that drift. The remaining innovations provide orthogonal increments whose squared amplitudes add to the variance. Applying the same construction to the physical perturbation makes response and fluctuations part of one covariance geometry. The usual uncertainty inequality then follows from Cauchy–Schwarz, and its equality condition specifies which observables retain all the dissipation information.

**Why it matters?** This is a substantial unification of finite-time response and thermodynamic inference, rather than simply another bound on one current. The preparation subtraction and explicit saturation conditions explain when an inferred dissipation bound is useful or loose. For nonequilibrium theory, the framework offers a systematic way to choose perturbations and observables appropriate to inertial dynamics; experimentally restricted measurements may access only part of that optimum.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 11

### Fast extraction of universal data from temporal spectrum: Applications to Haagerup CFT and monopole scattering
**Tokiro Numasawa, Boris De Vos, Masataka Watanabe, Atsushi Ueda — arXiv:2611.26257**

Extracting a conformal spectrum normally requires accurate critical ground states and costly calculations of excited states. Could a modest imaginary-time evolution expose the same universal information more directly? This paper uses space–time rotation of a tensor network to turn that evolution into a boundary-spectrum calculation.

The authors evolve simple boundary-state preparations as infinite matrix product states and diagonalize their transfer matrices. After geometric and velocity calibration, logarithms of the transfer eigenvalues yield conformal energy gaps in the rotated channel. The method resolves higher levels of the charge-resolved spectrum of a Haagerup anyon chain and estimates $c=2.137(27)$. It also realizes the Maldacena–Ludwig boundary associated with monopole scattering using a Fidkowski–Kitaev preparation, checking both identical- and mixed-boundary spectra.

**Background.** Open–closed duality views one Euclidean path integral either as a thermal trace on an interval or as propagation between boundary states on a circle. Rotating the tensor network exchanges these descriptions: the imaginary-time thickness becomes a spatial interval width. This explains why a transfer spectrum of an evolved state can encode a physical boundary spectrum. The Haagerup chain has an exotic fusion-category symmetry whose continuum identification remains unsettled. In the second application, an interaction among eight Majoranas provides an on-site entangled starting state; after rotation it supplies an interacting fermionic boundary condition rather than a conventional free boundary.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful computational bridge between lattice models and boundary CFT, especially where directly targeting many excited states becomes expensive. The unusual examples make the method more interesting than a benchmark on familiar minimal models. Its effectiveness still depends on boundary selection and control of finite-imaginary-time and bond-dimension errors. The Haagerup data constrain candidate theories rather than completing their identification.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 12

### Stringy Corrections from Gravity: Predictions for BFSS and ABJM
**Aidan Herderschee, Victor Ivo, Henry W. Lin, Yifan Wang — arXiv:2611.25022**

Comparisons between holography and simulations of strongly coupled quantum theories require corrections to the supergravity approximation. Even the leading finite-string-length contribution can be difficult to calculate when the full nonlinear effective action is unavailable. This paper obtains such information by exploiting the eleven-dimensional description instead.

The authors evaluate nonzero winding contributions to a one-loop supergravity calculation on a circle. Supersymmetric cancellations package the leading type IIA correction in the metric, dilaton, and Ramond–Ramond one-form sector into a finite supertrace over Lorentz generators. Applied to the thermal D0-brane background, it determines the first stringy correction to the low-temperature BFSS free energy. The inferred coefficient differs from published lattice estimates by roughly twenty percent, outside their reported errors. Extending the calculation to a background with four-form flux reproduces the known $\zeta(3)k^2$ contribution to the ABJM sphere free energy, where k is the Chern–Simons level.

**Background.** Compactifying M-theory on a circle gives type IIA string theory. Reorganizing a loop as paths winding around that circle separates terms with different dependence on the compactification scale. Supersymmetry relates bosonic and fermionic fluctuations, so their weighted trace cancels lower-derivative contributions and isolates the leading eight-derivative interaction. BFSS is supersymmetric matrix quantum mechanics, while ABJM is a three-dimensional Chern–Simons-matter theory with localization results that provide an independent check.

**Why it matters?** This is a concrete calculational advance with a quantitative target for many-body simulations of holographic theories. The discrepancy is useful because it puts pressure on identifiable numerical systematics, including low-temperature extrapolation and phase quenching, without by itself deciding their origin. The method’s broader value is extracting physically testable corrections from duality and loop structure when a direct string calculation is impractical.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 13

### Stitching Quantum Codes into Quilts: Universal Logic with no Ancillary Qubits
**Stasiu Wolanski — arXiv:2611.26329**

Efficient memory is only part of the challenge for quantum codes: joint logical measurements must also avoid consuming the qubits saved by a compact encoding. This paper asks whether existing code blocks can be coupled directly, using their ordinary syndrome ancillas, to measure a desired logical parity.

A code quilt decomposes the target logical product into smaller cross-block “stitching” operators. Together with the original checks, these define a subsystem code whose central operators include the target measurement. Wolanski constructs circuits using no physical qubits beyond those already required for idle syndrome extraction, and gives sufficient conditions for preserving code-capacity operational distance. Large searches find distance-preserving constructions for surface and bivariate bicycle codes, with selected circuit simulations giving logical error probabilities comparable to idle memory.

**Background.** The stitching operators can anticommute with original stabilizers. A subsystem formulation treats these noncommuting measurements as gauge information while protecting their commuting central products. For example, pairwise physical parities can multiply to the joint logical parity. One protocol measures the gauge generators directly; another fixes the stitching gauges and measures a resulting stabilizer code. Operational distance must protect both the remaining quantum state and the correctness of the recorded logical measurement, since either can fail independently.

**Why it matters?** This supplies a concrete approach to logical operations with little additional qubit overhead across different code families. Its limitations are central to the assessment: the direct-gauge protocol is provably not fault tolerant asymptotically, and general fault tolerance of the alternative is unproved. The finite-size results may still be useful for practical architectures. The deeper question is whether temporal protection and measurement design can retain that resource advantage as code distance grows.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 14

### High-Accuracy Neural-Network Quantum States via Randomized Real-Time Dynamics
**John M. Martyn, Rod Rofougaran, Samuel Stein, Di Luo — arXiv:2611.26587**

An excellent variational energy does not guarantee equally accurate correlation functions. The energy has no matrix element between the ground state and an orthogonal excited component, whereas a general observable does. Its error can therefore remain linear in a small wavefunction contamination even when the energy error is quadratic.

The authors improve observable estimates by averaging real-time evolution over randomly chosen durations. Relative phases between the ground state and excited components wash out, eliminating the leading interference error when the time distribution sufficiently resolves the excitation energies. The resulting mixed-state estimate can achieve quadratic error suppression without further energy minimization or a larger neural network. Tests use nonintegrable spin models. A complementary approximation expresses the correction through nested commutators, avoiding explicit evolution under an additional assumption that two eigenstates dominate.

**Background.** Real-time averaging dephases an approximate state in the energy basis; it does not reduce excited-state populations or the energy. A small excited amplitude produces a first-order off-diagonal contribution to an observable, but only a second-order population weight. Removing the former leaves the latter as the natural error floor. The Fourier transform of the time-sampling distribution determines which energy coherences survive. Small gaps demand longer averaging, and inaccurate simulated dynamics can reintroduce errors at the order one hoped to remove.

**Why it matters?** This is a useful method for improving the physical content of variational calculations after energy optimization has stalled. Its mechanism is general enough to interest users of other approximate wavefunctions as well as neural states. The relevant practical comparison includes the cost and accuracy of dynamics, or the stability of the commutator approximation; the rigorous dephasing argument alone does not make those steps inexpensive. It offers a promising connection between nonequilibrium evolution and ground-state numerical accuracy.

## arXiv-2026-11-20 — Item 15

### Tunable Ground-State Entanglement in Fully Connected Quantum Systems: From Logarithmic to Volume-Law Scaling
**Donghoon Kim, Tomotaka Kuwahara — arXiv:2611.26430**

All-to-all connectivity does not uniquely determine how entangled a gapped ground state can be. The normalization of each weak interaction matters, because there are increasingly many interactions per spin. This paper asks how sharply an entanglement bound changes when that normalization is relaxed.

Kim and Kuwahara construct a bipartite spin model with Fourier-patterned pair creation and annihilation, with pairwise strengths scaling as $n^{-\kappa}$ for n qubits. At sufficiently small fixed coupling they prove
$S=\Theta(n^{2-2\kappa}\log n)$ for $1/2<\kappa\leq1$,
and $S=\Theta(n)$ at $\kappa=1/2$.
The two-sided scaling bounds hold for every pure ground state. Uniqueness and a size-independent gap are proved for $3/4\leq\kappa\leq1$; the larger range has supporting weak-coupling and numerical evidence for the gap.

**Background.** Kac normalization, here $\kappa=1$, keeps the sum of interaction norms incident on one spin bounded as the system grows. Reducing the exponent lets that sum grow even though each pair coupling still vanishes. The Fourier phases distribute pairing over many modes. At weak excitation density, a bosonic approximation reorganizes them into independent two-mode squeezed pairs, making the accumulation of entanglement intuitive. The rigorous spin bounds establish the scaling without relying solely on that approximation. The notation Theta means matching upper and lower asymptotic bounds at fixed coupling and exponent.

**Why it matters?** This sharply identifies interaction normalization as an essential assumption in logarithmic entanglement bounds for fully connected systems. Even an arbitrarily small fixed decrease from the Kac exponent permits superlogarithmic entanglement in a rigorously gapped family. That helps clarify which intuitions about simple gapped states survive in nonlocal quantum simulators. The volume-law endpoint is established for entropy, while a rigorous uniform gap there remains open.
